## 1. The theme in one minute
<!-- readings: Schlaile et al. 2017, p. 4; Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 17 -->

In plain words, this theme is about direction. An innovation system can be busy, fast and clever, and still move in a direction nobody chose. Sustainability asks who chooses.

Schlaile and colleagues, 2017, make the point sharply. The usual view treats innovation as desirable in itself and mostly technological. They want a shift at the level of assumptions, a change in the beliefs that decide what counts as progress.

The theme also gives you a working definition of the goal. Fløysand and colleagues, 2022, citing UNEP, describe green industrial restructuring as change that cuts emissions and pollution, raises resource efficiency and protects biodiversity.

Why does the course care? Because the exam asks how systems promote or inhibit sustainable transformation. You cannot answer that without saying what transformation is for, and who decides. A system that is merely efficient can make the wrong thing faster.

Remember: sustainability is a direction, and a direction has to be chosen by someone.

## 2. The key ideas
<!-- readings: Schlaile et al. 2017, pp. 2, 5 -->

Four ideas carry the theme.

The first is that wicked problems do not yield to technology alone. Schlaile and colleagues, 2017, argue that such problems have emergent, socially embedded causes, and that they need change in the economy, in institutions, in technology, in culture and in organisation.

The second is the dedicated innovation system. The authors take the term from Pyka and give it a direction. A dedicated system is one that exists to serve transformations towards sustainability, rather than innovation in general.

The third is transformative innovation. Citing Steward, the authors describe it as novelty that breaks the frame and changes the whole system, beyond new products or processes.

The fourth is transformational failures. Citing Weber and Rohracher, they name the failures that a sustainability policy must also address, such as weak direction, weak legitimacy or weak responsibility.

Put them together. A wicked problem needs more than a device, and a system that serves sustainability has to be judged by its direction and not only by its output.

The authors also review the strands that try to answer the question. Transformative innovation policy, foresight, the entrepreneurial state, green industrial policy, inclusive systems, reflexive governance, transitions research and responsible innovation. Their verdict is that the strands are scattered. Each one holds a piece, and none of them puts direction at the centre.

Remember: wicked problems, a dedicated system, transformative innovation and transformational failures.

## 3. The model, step by step
<!-- readings: Schlaile et al. 2017, pp. 5, 6, 7 -->

The model is a set of three questions, and you can hear them as a checklist.

First, directionality. What should be transformed, and what should be sustained? This sounds easy and is not. The debate often assumes we already know the right direction, and that assumption clashes with open ended change and with conflicting interests.

Second, legitimacy. Why should this direction be followed, and who decides? Top down steering is hard in a network society, and bottom up participation cannot guarantee a good answer either.

Third, responsibility. By and for whom? Corporate responsibility and liability were built for single actors with clear outcomes, and they fit collective and uncertain outcomes badly.

The three questions are the model. Ask them in order, and a vague wish to be sustainable turns into a set of decisions that can be argued about.

There is one more feature worth hearing. The questions are hard in a particular way. Direction clashes with open ended change, legitimacy clashes with the speed of decisions, and responsibility clashes with the fact that outcomes are collective. That is why the authors call these failures and not problems to be solved once.

Remember: what to transform, who decides, and who answers for the result.

## 4. Norway examples
<!-- readings: Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15; Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 442 -->

The Norwegian examples show a direction that is agreed in words and contested in practice.

In Hordaland and Rogaland, Fløysand and colleagues, 2022, find two regions that both aim at a green shift, and oil and gas still dominates their business. That is the theme in one case. The goal is shared, and the direction of the system is not.

Fagerberg and colleagues, 2009, add the long view. Rapid growth from offshore oil and gas is approaching its end, and so is the era of cheap hydropower. The authors treat that as a warning, because a system built on one resource has to find another direction before the resource runs out.

Use the two together. The first shows that a shared vision is not enough, and the second shows why the question is urgent rather than academic.

Remember: a shared green vision, a petroleum system that continues, and an economic base that is running out.

## 5. How the exam asks about it
<!-- readings: Schlaile et al. 2017, p. 10; Class 9, slide 31 -->

The discussion question of December 2024 asks how technologies in innovation systems can promote or inhibit sustainable transformation.

Here is the shape of a good answer. Open by saying that a technology has no direction on its own, and that the system around it supplies one. Define the dedicated system, and name transformative innovation. Then bring in the three questions, because they turn the case into an argument.

For the case, use the two regions. A technology can promote transformation when the institutional work around it points the same way. It can inhibit transformation when the system keeps rewarding the old path, as petroleum still does in both regions.

One sentence about the authors helps. Schlaile and colleagues, 2017, say that a shift at the level of assumptions is the only way to bring the normativity of sustainability into the system. Say it in your own words, and then show it in Norway.

Remember: technologies take their direction from the system, and the system can point either way, even when everyone says the same good words.

## 6. Test yourself
<!-- readings: Schlaile et al. 2017, pp. 5, 10; Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15 -->

Question one. What are the three questions a dedicated system has to answer?

Pause the audio and answer in your head.

Here is the answer. Directionality, which asks what to transform and what to sustain. Legitimacy, which asks why and who decides. Responsibility, which asks by and for whom.

Question two. What is a transformational failure?

Pause the audio and answer in your head.

Here is the answer. It is a failure that a sustainability policy has to address beyond the usual market failures, such as a weak sense of direction, weak legitimacy or weak responsibility.

Question three. Why is a shared green vision not enough?

Pause the audio and answer in your head.

Here is the answer. Because a vision does not change a system by itself. In Hordaland and Rogaland the vision was shared, and oil and gas still dominated, because the rules and the rewards still pointed that way.

Remember: ask what to change, who decides and who answers, and then look at what the system actually rewards, because the reward is usually the real answer.
