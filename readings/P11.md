## 1. The paper in brief
<!-- pages: all -->

The paper asks three questions about a literature that has grown quickly. How has research on crowdfunding and innovation evolved? Which fields and research streams have emerged from the two? And which questions are still open (page 1111)? It matters because earlier mappings of crowdfunding took single aspects, such as one campaign model or the success factors of a campaign. None framed the knowledge structure of the whole field.

The authors treat crowdfunding as more than a source of money. On page 1110 they argue that it is also a valid instrument to foster innovation. Collective financing lets users act like venture capitalists. Crowdfunding spread for two reasons: it finances innovation at lower cost than traditional finance, and it tests projects outside the organisation. Three concepts carry the analysis. Innovativeness comes in two forms. On page 1115, following Chan and Parhankangas, incremental innovativeness is a cumulative improvement of existing technology, knowledge and capabilities. Radical innovativeness is more drastic, unique and comprehensive. Openness comes from open innovation. Following Stanko and Henard, crowdfunding can be viewed as an embodiment of that paradigm, since it seeks ideas from outside (page 1116). Social capital is the third, defined on page 1117 through Nahapiet and Ghoshal as the resources available through the network of relationships of an individual or social unit.

The method is a bibliometric review. The authors searched Elsevier's Scopus in January 2022. They looked for articles with crowdfunding terms and innovation terms in the title, abstract or keywords. A PRISMA diagram took 2,544 records down to 150 studies (page 1112). They analysed those 150 with VosViewer and the Bibliometrix package in R. Three tools did the work: co-citation of cited references, co-occurrence of author keywords, and a conceptual thematic map (page 1113). The map sorts themes by Callon's centrality and density. The articles were written by 379 authors in 115 journals, with 31 citations per document on average (page 1114).

The co-citation network gives three clusters (page 1121). They are crowdfunding as a source of radical and incremental innovation, crowdfunding and the openness construct, and crowdfunding and platform innovation. The keyword analysis gives seven, from crowdfunding and innovation through knowledge to crowdfunding, innovation and social media (page 1119). The thematic map puts reward-based crowdfunding among the motor themes. Crowdfunding, fintech and equity crowdfunding sit among the basic and transversal themes, and venture financing among the niche themes. Finance is left isolated among the emerging themes (page 1120). The paper reports that 2018 and 2019 were the strongest years, with 64 articles. Ordanini et al. is the most cited work, with 544 citations (page 1114). The paper closes with 25 research questions in three clusters (page 1123).

The authors call this the first study to combine content analysis with bibliometric analysis on these two fields (page 1121). They say it offers a holistic view and a research agenda (page 1127). They name two limits. Scopus was the only source, so work indexed elsewhere, such as Web of Science, may be missing. Articles in languages other than English were excluded (page 1127).

The paper is the reading for the bonus lecture, The art of crowdfunding. That lecture uses the thematic map from page 1120 to explain motor, basic, emerging and niche themes. The lecture notes that crowdfunding was not on the December 2024 or the June 2025 exam. Question 2 of December 2024 asked how agency in innovation systems can promote or inhibit sustainable transformation. Our Bonus lecture page suggests, as an example of our own rather than the lecturer's, that crowdfunding communities could be one form of such agency. Here is an example of our own, not from the paper: a small firm could use backers' pledges to show demand for a green product. The paper's nearest point, on page 1125, is that SMEs and MNEs may use crowdfunding to reduce uncertainty about demand.

## 2. The question, the three research questions and the search
<!-- pages: 1109-1112 -->

### Page 1109
This bibliometric review of the literature linking crowdfunding and innovation appears in the European Journal of Innovation Management. The abstract gives the purpose: to identify, evaluate and synthesise earlier research on the intertwining of the two fields. It reports a meta-analysis of 150 articles from Scopus, analysed with VosViewer and the Bibliometrix package in R, and names three clusters: radical and incremental innovation, openness, and platform innovation. The introduction opens with pooling resources, which has let entrepreneurs start businesses and large companies launch products with less risk of go-to-market failure.

### Page 1110
The introduction argues that crowdfunding is not only a way to raise money but also a valid instrument to foster innovation. Choosing which innovation to develop and commercialise is usually made by a small group of experts, or by venture capitalists in a start-up. Users of technology, rather than manufacturers, create key innovations, and collective financing lets users act like venture capitalists. Crowdfunding spread for two reasons: it finances innovation at lower cost than traditional finance, and it tests projects outside the organisation. Earlier mappings covered single aspects, so the authors ask how the literature has evolved.

### Page 1111
Two more questions follow: which fields and research streams have emerged, and which future questions could develop the field. The method is a systematic review of work from 2010 to 2022, using co-citation analysis, keyword co-occurrence, thematic analysis and keyword analysis in VosViewer and Bibliometrix. The authors chose Scopus for its quality standards, its coverage of 1.7 billion cited references from 1970, and its business and management journals. The search ran in January 2022, combining crowdfunding terms with innovation, R&D, research and development and open innovation.

### Page 1112
Figure 1 is a PRISMA flow diagram. It starts with 2,544 articles retrieved by the database search. English language leaves 2,487. Two subject areas, business, management and accounting, and economics, econometrics and finance, leave 933. Peer-reviewed articles leave 888. Reading titles and abstracts leaves 410. The total included in the quantitative synthesis is 150. The text adds that the search was not restricted by date, that conference proceedings and books were excluded, and that unrelated and duplicate articles were removed. The section then introduces VosViewer for the citation and keyword networks.

## 3. The three tools, the publication trends and the first two clusters
<!-- pages: 1113-1116 -->

### Page 1113
Three tools carry the analysis. Co-citation rests on a point from Ziman: "a scientific paper does not stand alone; it is embedded in the literature of the subject". It can be run on authors, journals or articles, and, citing Akbari and colleagues, it is called the most popular relational technique in bibliometric studies. Keyword co-occurrence clusters words used most often together, sizing nodes by weight. The thematic map sorts themes by Callon's centrality, the strength of external ties, and density, the internal strength. The results open with 150 articles by 379 authors in 115 journals, averaging 31 citations each.

### Page 1114
Table 1 gives 2010 to 2022, 115 sources, 150 documents, 8,854 references, 464 author keywords, 379 authors and 22 single-authored documents, at 31 citations per document. Figure 2 plots total studies per year and a second line labelled TC/TS. Following Shneor and Vik, the text links the 2010 start to two platforms, Prosper for peer-to-peer lending in 2005 and Kickstarter for reward crowdfunding in 2009. Output in 2013 was five times the year before, and 2018 and 2019 were the strongest years with 64 articles, 32 of them in 2019. Ordanini et al. is the most cited, with 544 citations.

<!-- sourced: 8854 | page 1114, Table 1 row "References", printed there as 8,854 -->

### Page 1115
Figure 3 is the co-citation network: three coloured clusters joined through one central paper by Mollick and Nanda. Cluster A is crowdfunding as a source of radical and incremental innovation. Chan and Parhankangas separate incremental innovativeness, a cumulative improvement of existing technology, knowledge and capabilities, from radical innovativeness, which is more drastic, unique and comprehensive. Their findings suggest that backers join campaigns they see as familiar and feasible, while campaigns that demand more effort to understand are riskier. The page also separates crowdfunding as finance for innovation from crowdfunding as the crowd's way into the innovation process.

### Page 1116
Managing information to the public is a strategic competence, and an open call can show whether an entrepreneur meets delivery deadlines. But feedback can overwhelm a manager and take unsustainable time, while ignoring it can backfire in negative postings. Crowdfunding's contribution to innovation thus goes beyond money, to the crowd's participation. Cluster A closes on green innovation, where a sustainability orientation can help raise funds. Cluster B opens on openness. Following Stanko and Henard, crowdfunding embodies open innovation; they find that open search depth helps product market performance, while breadth pushes a radical focus.

## 4. Social capital, platform innovation, the keyword clusters and the thematic map
<!-- pages: 1117-1120 -->

### Page 1117
Cluster B turns to social capital, defined through Nahapiet and Ghoshal as the resources available through the network of relationships of an individual or social unit. Skirnevskyi et al. find that resources from earlier campaigns shape later ones, and that serial creators replace family and friends with the platform community to sustain success beyond one campaign. Disclosure is meant to limit adverse selection. Cluster C opens with platforms as intermediaries in a two-sided market, where project owners subsidise the platform and contributors are subsidised, and with Ordanini et al.'s experience investors, drawn by the novelty of the tool.

### Page 1118
The page adds that crowdfunders cannot always judge a project, so Parker notes that uninformed investors follow the informed few. Burtch et al. find that hiding information about earlier contributors lowers later contributions. Social media then enter: Vismara associates many friends on Facebook, Twitter or Instagram with success, and the page describes platforms as social network hubs. The keyword analysis follows, on articles with at least seven keyword occurrences, sorted into seven clusters. Crowdfunding and innovation are the largest nodes. The first cluster is crowdfunding and innovation through knowledge.

### Page 1119
Figure 4 is the keyword co-occurrence network, and Table 2 sets out the seven clusters with their items and topics. Cluster I is crowdfunding and innovation through knowledge, with fintech, patents and professional investors among its keywords. Cluster II is intellectual capital, cluster III technological innovation, with blockchain and mechanism design, cluster IV civic innovation, cluster V open innovation, cluster VI business innovation, and cluster VII crowdfunding, innovation and social media. The page adds that only keywords appearing at least three times were included.

### Page 1120
Figure 5 is the conceptual thematic map, with density on the vertical axis and centrality on the horizontal. Motor themes, high on both, are led by reward-based crowdfunding, which is tied to reducing information asymmetry and can act as a pre-test before commercialisation. Basic and transversal themes have low density and high centrality and include crowdfunding, fintech and equity crowdfunding, a tool for long-term capital spending on projects that need time. Emerging themes are weak on both, and finance sits isolated there. Niche themes have high density but low centrality, such as venture financing.

## 5. The discussion and what the clusters mean for managers
<!-- pages: 1121-1123 -->

### Page 1121
The discussion answers the first question: research began in 2010 and grew until 2018 and 2019, the strongest years. The authors call this the first study to combine content analysis with bibliometric analysis on these two fields. The citation network yields the three clusters, and the page restates the first. Metrick and Yasuda had suggested that equity investors prefer extremely innovative ventures, but the crowdfunding audience is wider than professional investors. Incremental campaigns are more comprehensible and create more user value, while radical ones are riskier and attract less participation.

### Page 1122
The page finishes cluster B: external social capital matters early, internal social capital grows across the campaign. The third cluster follows, where social media spread the campaign, reduce uncertainty and even help predict success. It then lists the seven topics found by keyword analysis. The thematic map puts equity crowdfunding and innovation in the mainstream and leaves finance and signalling to be developed. A strategic diagram traces three sub-periods, 2010 to 2014, 2015 to 2018 and 2019 to 2022, where kick-starter and start-up disappear while open innovation arrives. An analysis of countries, authors and keywords finds China the largest contributor.

### Page 1123
The three clusters give crowdfunders and platform managers cues on using crowdfunding to stimulate innovation, and on how innovation attracts both sides of a platform. Cluster A helps entrepreneurs present a campaign: incremental innovativeness reads as more comprehensible, radical as riskier. Cluster B speaks to managers who want innovation with less risk than doing it all internally, and to social capital. For cluster C, the authors posit that links with social media could help project performance. The future research section reports 25 questions in Table 3. Financing innovation is hard because backers must be convinced and their uncertainty lowered.

## 6. Twenty-five questions for future research
<!-- pages: 1124-1126 -->

### Page 1124
Table 3, over two pages, sets questions from earlier work beside proposed avenues. Cluster A asks how consumers react to incremental and radical innovativeness, how entrepreneurial orientation affects early fundraising, and whether crowdfunding raises innovation. Its avenues compare professional investors with crowdfunders, and look at start-ups, SMEs and MNEs, backer interaction and green projects. Cluster B asks about information quality, other meanings of radicalness, openness in small firms, kinds of updates and cross-national differences. Its avenues cover openness and market performance, disclosure and adverse selection, niche platforms and backer comments.

### Page 1125
The page returns to uncertainty. Platforms could profile users by their appetite for more or less innovative projects, and target them better. The page contrasts start-ups with SMEs and MNEs. Start-ups come for scarce resources. SMEs and MNEs may come to reduce uncertainty about aggregate demand and to de-risk a launch. The page also stresses the two-way flow of information: more backers bring more ideas to work with. That opens questions on managerial competences. The cluster C block closes the table, on platform innovation, influencer endorsement, blockchain and longitudinal work on success factors.

### Page 1126
The text asks which managerial competences a campaign needs; SMEs and MNEs are less studied than enthusiastic founders. Green innovation is hard to finance for three reasons: risky projects, backers' lack of technical knowledge, and consumers who are not ready. Since adoption speed and scale matter more than the innovation itself, the authors suggest research on subsidies. For cluster B, crowdfunding is a setting where technology opens firms to outside knowledge, linking initiators, pledgers, donors and platforms. Open questions include openness and market performance, disclosure, online communities and niche platforms. Cluster C begins with integration with social media.

## 7. The limitations and the reference list
<!-- pages: 1127-1133 -->

### Page 1127
The page closes cluster C with influencer endorsement, which reaches large audiences and, through self-image congruence, raises willingness to fund. Blockchain appears as a way to secure data management in equity crowdfunding, and as a tool policy makers could use for traceability. The limitations follow. Scopus was the only source, so work indexed elsewhere, such as Web of Science, may be missing, and articles in other languages were left out; future work could add Spanish, Italian and French. Despite these limits, the authors say the analysis offers an overall view and a research agenda. The reference list then begins.
