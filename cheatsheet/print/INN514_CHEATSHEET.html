Promote or inhibit

  • Promotes: related variety lets a region recombine what it has into a new path. (Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 330)
  • Inhibits: a specialized, dirigiste system can keep a region in one industry. (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15)

Theme 1: Innovation systems and Norway's national innovation system

Core definitions

  • Innovation system (innovasjonssystem): learning and innovation across the whole economy, not only in universities and research institutes. (Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 431)
  • National innovation system (nasjonalt innovasjonssystem): the same at the scale of a country, a term the authors trace to Freeman, Lundvall and Nelson. (Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 431)
  • Institutions (institusjoner): citing North, the rules of the game, costly to establish but scale-giving once adopted. (Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 433)
  • Path dependency (stiavhengighet): historical processes that favour some solutions and hold others back. (Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 431)
  • Trinity of innovation (treenighet): citing Schwarz and Thompson, the patterns of people, things and ideas behind technology. (Fløysand et al. 2013, PDF p. 9)

Key models and figures

Three industrial development paths (Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 439):

  1. Small-scale decentralized path: small firms investing little of their own funds.
  2. Large-scale centralized path: metals, electrochemistry and hydropower, often foreign-financed.
  3. Knowledge-intensive network-based path: R&D-intensive firms in sectors such as ICT.

The pragmatic innovation complex (Fløysand et al. 2013, PDF p. 10):

  1. Company and tools meet through technique.
  2. Tools and tasks meet through production, with unintended outputs.
  3. Company and tasks meet through rationale, profit.

Exam angles

  • Dec 2024 Q1: the four terms, each defined and tied to Norway, with one source per term. (our reading)
  • Class 9 example: how innovation systems promote or inhibit sustainable transformation. [Class 9, slide 38]
  • Practice: use the petroleum suppliers as the example. (ours)

Norway examples

  • Domestic petroleum suppliers: shipbuilding, engineering and services grew with petroleum instead of Dutch disease. (Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 434)
  • The concession laws: early waterfall rules became the model for national control of offshore oil and gas, decades later. (Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 441)
  • Fish-farming: disease control was mastered without a large corporate laboratory, building on regional resources. (Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 441)

  • Theory: Fagerberg et al. (2009) use a broad definition of the national innovation system, where learning happens across the whole economy. Citing North, they treat institutions as the rules of the game, and they show how path dependency keeps a system on its track. Fløysand et al. (2013) add the trinity of actors, objects and ideas.

Theme 2: Regional innovation systems and industrial restructuring

Core definitions

  • Regional innovation system (regionalt innovasjonssystem): citing Cooke, interacting subsystems of knowledge infrastructure and commercial exploitation, set in knowledge, industry and governance. (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 16)
  • Related variety (relatert variasjon): industries that differ but are related, which gives a region room to combine them and innovate across industries. (Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 330)
  • Path extension (baneforlengelse): more of the same, continuing an existing industrial path rather than opening a new one. (Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 328)
  • Path creation (baneskaping): developing a new industry rather than extending an old one. (Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 328)
  • Institutional work (institusjonelt arbeid): citing Lawrence and colleagues, the work actors do to affect institutions, whether maintaining, creating, changing or disrupting them. (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15)

Key models and figures

Three levels of regional renewal (Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 324):

  1. Industry level: cluster dynamics, how firms and support institutions interact.
  2. Firm level: related variety across industries.
  3. Individual level: entrepreneurship, the actors who start something new.

Four industrial trajectories (Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 328):

  1. Path exhaustion: an industry weakens and loses its base.
  2. Path extension: more of the same.
  3. Path renewal: upgrading the industry with new products.
  4. Path creation: a new industry.

Exam angles

  • Jun 2025 Q2: how a regional system promotes or inhibits restructuring, with examples from the two regions. (our reading)
  • Jun 2025 Q1: the regional system and institutional work, defined through the two regions. (our reading)

Norway examples

  • Hordaland: a diversified, interactive system where collective work legitimizes a green shift. (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15)
  • Rogaland: a specialized, dirigiste system where individual work delegitimizes it. (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15)
  • Mongstad microalgae: a pilot that would join oil knowledge to marine knowledge in fish feed. (Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 337)

  • Theory: Cooke's definition treats a regional innovation system as interacting subsystems of knowledge and exploitation. Fløysand and Jakobsen (2016) add related variety: industries that differ but are related give a region room to combine them, and so to renew without starting from nothing.

Theme 3: Institutions and institutional work

Core definitions

  • Institutions (institusjoner): citing North, the formal and informal rules that shape action, both written rules and informal ways of doing. (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 16)
  • Institutional work (institusjonelt arbeid): citing Lawrence and colleagues, the physical and mental work actors do to affect institutions, whether maintaining, creating, changing or disrupting them. (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15)
  • Institutional logics (institusjonelle logikker): citing Thornton and Almandoz, practice patterns and social prescriptions that legitimize practice, and so direct what actors see as possible. (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 18)
  • Change agency (endringsaktørskap): following Bækkelund, the agency that alters a path. (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 17)
  • Reproductive agency (reproduktivt aktørskap): following Bækkelund, the agency that keeps a path going. (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 17)

Key models and figures

Four forms of institutional work, citing Lawrence and colleagues (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 17):

  1. Maintaining institutions: keeping rules in place.
  2. Creating new institutions: building rules that did not exist.
  3. Changing existing institutions: reshaping what is there.
  4. Disrupting institutions: breaking them down.

The lock-in cycle in Rogaland (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 24):

  1. A recession lowers costs in the petroleum sector.
  2. Oil prices recover.
  3. High profit margins return to the sector.
  4. The sector bounces back and the region stays on its path.

  • Rogaland: individual leadership, linked to Arne Rettedal and the petroleum headquarters in Stavanger. (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 22)
  • The dominance that lasts: oil and gas still dominates both regions, unintentionally, despite a shared green vision. [Class 9, slide 31]

Those efforts can maintain, create, change or disrupt them, and they work through institutional logics that decide what counts as legitimate.

Theme 4: Entrepreneurial ecosystems

Core definitions

  • Entrepreneurial ecosystem (entreprenørielt økosystem): citing Hawley, a system marked by co-evolution, mutualistic interdependence and diverse, nested actors, which begins when entrepreneurs' paths cross. (Stam & van de Ven 2021, p. 811)
  • Munificence (rikelighet): how many resources an ecosystem holds, from finance to talent. (Spigel & Harrison 2018, p. 161)
  • Functionality (funksjonalitet): how easily entrepreneurs can reach those resources, through networks and trust. (Spigel & Harrison 2018, p. 161)
  • Selection effects (seleksjonseffekter): opportunity costs and social legitimacy decide who becomes an entrepreneur, before any venture starts, through opportunity costs. (Autio et al. 2014, p. 1100)
  • Strategic choice effects (strategiske valgeffekter): what those who enter then judge desirable and feasible, once they have started. (Autio et al. 2014, p. 1100)

Key models and figures

Ten elements in three groups (Stam & van de Ven 2021, p. 813):

  1. Institutional arrangements: formal institutions, culture and networks that set the rules.
  2. Resources: infrastructure, finance, leadership, talent, knowledge, services and demand.
  3. Output: productive entrepreneurship, the firms that commercialise and grow.

Four ecosystem types (Spigel & Harrison 2018, p. 163):

  1. Strong: many resources, well-functioning networks, as in Silicon Valley.
  2. Arid: sparse resources, well-functioning networks, as in some developing economies.
  3. Irrigated: many resources, poorly functioning networks.
  4. Weak: sparse resources, poorly functioning networks, as in deindustrialised regions.

Norway examples

  • Jahnebakken in Bergen: an investor and founder house that gathers capital, mentors and new firms in one house. [Class 4, slide 19]
  • Work visas: Norway uses work visas as one immigration-policy lever for entrepreneurs, beside start-up visas in other countries. [Class 4, slide 17]

  • Theory: Stam and van de Ven (2021) sort the ecosystem into institutions, resources and output. Autio et al. (2014) add selection and strategic choice, which decide who enters and what they attempt, and so shape the rate of new ventures.

Theme 5: Agency and new path development

Core definitions

  • Agency (aktørskap): citing Sotarauta and Suvinen, action or intervention by an actor to produce a particular effect, whether intended or not. (Bækkelund 2021, p. 758)
  • Change agency (endringsaktørskap): following Bækkelund, the agency that alters a path, through entrepreneurship, institutional work or leadership. (Bækkelund 2021, p. 759)
  • Reproductive agency (reproduktivt aktørskap): following Bækkelund, the agency that keeps a path going, through replication, maintenance or institutional work. (Bækkelund 2021, p. 759)
  • Path creation (baneskaping): citing MacKinnon and colleagues, the emergence of new development trajectories in a region, such as a new industry. (Njøs et al. 2020, p. 268)
  • Bricolage (bricolage): citing Levi-Strauss, resourcefulness and improvisation with what is at hand, in modest and steady steps. (Garud & Karnøe 2003, p. 278)

Key models and figures

A range from change to reproductive agency (Bækkelund 2021, p. 760):

  1. Change: Schumpeterian entrepreneurship, new combinations.
  2. Change: institutional entrepreneurship, rewriting the rules.
  3. Change: place-based leadership, rooted in the place.
  4. Reproductive: replicative entrepreneurship, copying what works.
  5. Reproductive: institutional work.
  6. Reproductive: maintenance leadership, keeping the place steady.

Three modes of change (Miörner & Trippl 2017, p. 486):

  1. Layering: adding something new to the support structure.
  2. Adaptation: changing what exists.
  3. Novel application: reusing what exists in a new way.

Exam angles

  • Dec 2024 Q2: agency promotes or inhibits through change and reproductive work. (our reading)
  • Class 9 example: how innovation systems promote or inhibit sustainable transformation. [Class 9, slide 38]
  • Practice: compare Flåm and Sogndal on the same range of agency and modes of change. (ours)

Norway examples

  • Flåm, Odda and Sogndal: three fjord villages with the same institutions and landscape but different tourism paths, a most-similar design. (Bækkelund 2021, p. 761)
  • Flåm: more change agency than the other two villages. (Bækkelund 2021, p. 765)
  • Maritime batteries: a cluster set a direction and public procurement created the market. (Njøs et al. 2020, pp. 281–282)

Drafting aid (ours): learn the moves, then write your own words.

  • Intro: I read the question as asking how agency can push a system towards sustainability or hold it back, and I answer with two kinds of agency and one Norwegian case, the three fjord villages.
  • Theory: Bækkelund (2021) separates change agency, which alters a path, from reproductive agency, which keeps it going. Garud and Karnøe (2003) show that agency is distributed, so no single hero carries a new path alone.

Theme 6: Technological innovation systems and their functions

Core definitions

  • Technological innovation system (teknologisk innovasjonssystem): citing Carlsson and Stankiewicz, a network of agents that create, spread and use a technology under shared institutions. (Hekkert et al. 2007, p. 416)
  • Function (funksjon): an activity that results in technological change; Hekkert and colleagues list seven. (Hekkert et al. 2007, p. 415)
  • Guidance of the search (retning for søket): the function that sets expectations and goals and so steers where a technology goes. (Hekkert et al. 2007, p. 423)
  • Materialization (materialisering): the function that covers large physical infrastructure, added because CCS needs it. (Steen et al. 2024, p. 2)
  • Legitimation (legitimering): the function that covers acceptance and legitimacy. (Steen et al. 2024, p. 3)

Key models and figures

Seven functions of innovation systems (Hekkert et al. 2007, pp. 421–425):

  1. Entrepreneurial activity
  2. Knowledge development
  3. Knowledge diffusion through networks
  4. Guidance of the search
  5. Market formation
  6. Resource mobilization
  7. Creation of legitimacy

Three motors of change (Hekkert et al. 2007, p. 426):

  1. Motor A: lobbying for market formation, then entrepreneurial activity.
  2. Motor B: lobbying for research resources, then higher expectations.
  3. Motor C: guidance of the search, then resources and knowledge.

Theme 7: Sustainability, transformation and normative direction

Core definitions

  • Green industrial restructuring (grønn industriell omstilling): citing UNEP, change that cuts emissions and pollution, raises resource efficiency and protects biodiversity, such as a shift from petroleum. (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 17)
  • Paradigm (paradigme): a set of assumptions, concepts, values and practices that make up a shared worldview and span a bounded space of what counts as reasonable. (Schlaile et al. 2017, p. 4)
  • Dedicated innovation system (dedikert innovasjonssystem): from Pyka, an innovation system dedicated to transformations towards sustainability, which puts trajectories under a sustainability goal. (Schlaile et al. 2017, p. 4)
  • Transformative innovation (transformativ innovasjon): citing Steward, novelty that breaks the paradigm and changes the whole system, beyond new products or processes. (Schlaile et al. 2017, p. 5)
  • Transformational failures (transformasjonssvikt): citing Weber and Rohracher, the failures that a sustainability policy must also address, such as weak direction, legitimacy or responsibility. (Schlaile et al. 2017, p. 5)

Key models and figures

Three normative questions (Schlaile et al. 2017, pp. 5–7):

  1. Directionality: what to transform or sustain.
  2. Legitimacy: why, and who decides.
  3. Responsibility: by and for whom.

Exam angles

  • Dec 2024 Q3: a technology promotes or inhibits only inside a direction, with one example of each side. (our reading)

Norway examples

  • The end of cheap growth: rapid growth from offshore oil and gas is ending, and so is cheap hydropower, which the authors treat as a warning. (Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 442)
  • The dominance that lasts: oil and gas still dominates Hordaland and Rogaland, unintentionally, despite a shared green vision, which the authors call an unintended outcome. [Class 9, slide 31]

  • Theory: Schlaile et al. (2017) argue that a shift at the paradigm level is the only way to integrate the normativity of sustainability. A dedicated system then asks what to transform, who decides and who answers for the result, in that order.
  • Example: Fløysand et al. (2022) show that Hordaland and Rogaland both aim at a green shift, yet oil and gas still dominates their business. The same regions show that a technology promotes transformation only when the institutional work around it points the same way.
  • Mini-conclusion: A technology promotes transformation when it sits inside a dedicated system, and inhibits it when it only extends the old paradigm and its interests.

Theme 8: Crowdfunding and service ecosystems

Core definitions

  • Crowdfunding (folkefinansiering): collective financing that also works as an instrument to foster innovation, and lets users act like venture capitalists. (Bargoni et al. 2024, p. 1110)
  • Incremental innovativeness (inkrementell innovativitet): citing Chan and Parhankangas, a cumulative improvement of existing technology and capabilities, which builds on what a firm already does. (Bargoni et al. 2024, p. 1115)
  • Radical innovativeness (radikal innovativitet): citing Chan and Parhankangas, change that is more drastic, unique and comprehensive, and opens something genuinely new. (Bargoni et al. 2024, p. 1115)
  • Value proposition (verdiforslag): citing Frow and colleagues, a mechanism that adjusts as actors negotiate how resources are shared, so the offer changes as the relationship changes. (Quero & Ventura 2019, p. 52)
  • Crowdfunding ecosystem (folkefinansieringsøkosystem): a service ecosystem in which actors cocreate value to carry one actor's proposal forward, which makes backers, platforms and institutions one system. (Quero & Ventura 2019, p. 48)

Key models and figures

Five steps of value cocreation, adapting Frow and Payne (Quero & Ventura 2019, p. 51):

  1. Identify actors.
  2. Determine core values.
  3. Facilitate dialogue between the actors.
  4. Identify cocreation opportunities.
  5. Cocreate value propositions with the actors.

Six dimensions of each platform (Demattos Guimarães et al. 2024, p. 8):

  1. Country.
  2. Geographical scope.
  3. Business model.
  4. Focus of the platform.
  5. Institutional structure.
  6. Foundation year.

The relational forms of cultural-creative crowdfunding: a typology of practices through mapping platforms in Europe and Latin America, Poetics 105.

In one line

A typology of cultural-creative crowdfunding, built from 89 platforms in Europe and Latin America.

Key concepts

  • Cultural-creative crowdfunding (kultur-kreativ folkefinansiering): financing culture projects by asking a crowd for contributions through online platforms, a set of diverse practices. (p. 2)
  • Cultural policy paradigms (kulturpolitiske paradigmer): citing Bonet and Négrier, excellence, democratisation, cultural democracy and the creative economy. (p. 4)
  • Tracking and trawling (sporing og tråling): citing Whiting and Prichard, the digital method used to build the database. (p. 6)
  • Typology of practices (praksistypologi): the map from institutional structure to practices, the paper's main output. (p. 12)

Model

Six dimensions of each platform (p. 8):

  1. Country.
  2. Geographical scope.
  3. Business model.
  4. Focus.
  5. Institutional structure.
  6. Foundation year.

Findings

  • 67 of the 89 platforms were founded between 2009 and 2015. (p. 9)
  • 51 platforms use a hybrid business model, against 38 with a single one. (p. 9)
  • In Latin America no platform serves a single sector, while European platforms are more often regional. (p. 12)

Norway link

  • No Norwegian case in this paper.

Exam use

  • Dec 2024 Q2: crowdfunding communities as agency that moves resources away from gatekeepers. (our reading)

Limits

  • The study relies on what platforms display, and that information is inconsistent. (p. 8)
  • US platforms such as Kickstarter are left out, and a North American study is invited. (p. 10)

Hook

Eighty-nine platforms, six dimensions, one typology of practices.

Paper 13: Quero & Ventura (2019)

Value proposition as a framework for value cocreation in crowdfunding ecosystems, Marketing Theory 19(1).

In one line

The authors treat crowdfunding as a service ecosystem and map how actors cocreate its value propositions.

Key concepts

  • Crowdfunding ecosystem (folkefinansieringsøkosystem): a service ecosystem in which actors cocreate value to carry one actor's proposal forward. (p. 48)
  • Value proposition (verdiforslag): citing Frow and colleagues, a mechanism that adjusts as actors negotiate how resources are shared. (p. 52)
  • The Eight Co's (de åtte co-ene): the eight ways a value proposition is cocreated, an extension of five earlier categories. (p. 53)
  • Micro, meso and macro levels (mikro-, meso- og makronivå): citing Chandler and Vargo, direct exchange, third-party exchange and networks. (p. 55)

Model

Five steps of value cocreation, adapting Frow and Payne (p. 51):

  1. Identify actors.
  2. Determine core values.
  3. Facilitate dialogue between the actors.
  4. Identify cocreation opportunities.
  5. Cocreate value propositions with the actors.

Findings

  • The two directors added public institutions, a new actor in the ecosystem. (p. 58)
  • Across the three levels the authors find eight forms of cocreation in six value proposition metaphors. (p. 60)
  • At the macro level, associations and public institutions bring institutionalisation, a possibly conflictual form of cocreation. (p. 58)

Norway link

  • No Norwegian case in this paper.

Exam use

  • Dec 2024 Q2: crowdfunding communities as a service ecosystem. (our reading)

Limits

  • The sampling could broaden the kinds of cultural project and the countries studied. (p. 59)
  • Checking the model outside the cultural setting would be interesting. (p. 59)

Hook

Eight actors, three levels, one proposal carried forward.

Answer plans for the six past questions

Drafting aid (ours): learn the moves, then write your own words. Each plan is voiced as a worked answer on the companion site (Revision 11 to 16).

December 2024, Question 1

Question: What do you understand by innovation system? What do you understand by sustainable transformation? What do you understand by "agency"? What do you understand by "institutions"? Thesis: Define each term from the course, name its source, and show one Norwegian case. Moves: 1. Define the broad system (Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 431) 2. Use the UNEP change line (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 17) 3. Separate two kinds of agency (Bækkelund 2021, p. 758) 4. Call institutions rules of the game (Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 431) 5. Join them in the two regions (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15) Example: In Hordaland collective work legitimised a green shift, and in Rogaland individual work did not (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15). Trap: Do not use a present-needs sentence as the definition of sustainable transformation.

December 2024, Question 2

Question: How can "agency" in innovation systems promote or inhibit sustainable transformation? Use examples. Thesis: Agency can open a greener path or keep an old path in place. Moves: 1. Read both sides of the command [Class 9, slide 39] 2. Define both kinds of agency (Bækkelund 2021, p. 758) 3. Show how change work promotes (Bækkelund 2021, p. 758) 4. Show how reproductive work inhibits (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 22) 5. Use the two regions, then close (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15) Example: Collective work in Hordaland legitimised a green shift, while individual leadership in Rogaland did not (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15). Trap: Do not call either kind of agency good or bad.

December 2024, Question 3

Question: How can technologies in innovation systems promote or inhibit sustainable transformation? Use examples. Thesis: A technology helps when direction and a market are built, and hinders when those functions stay weak. Moves: 1. Read both sides of the command [Class 9, slide 39] 2. Define the system and functions (Hekkert et al. 2007, p. 421) 3. Show a direction and a market (Njøs et al. 2020, p. 281) 4. Show weak functions that inhibit (Steen et al. 2024, p. 9) 5. Use batteries and capture, then close (Steen et al. 2024, p. 4) Example: A cluster set a battery direction, and public procurement created the market (Njøs et al. 2020, p. 281). Trap: Do not treat knowledge and legitimation as strengths of capture.

June 2025, Question 1

Question: What do you understand by a regional innovation system? What do you understand by institutional work? What do you understand by sustainability? Thesis: Define each term from the course, name its source, and show the two regions. Moves: 1. Define the regional system (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 16) 2. Name the four trajectories (Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 328) 3. Define institutional work (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15) 4. Use the three UN lines [Class 9, slide 29] 5. Show the two directions of work (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 22) Example: Collective work in diversified Hordaland legitimised a green shift, unlike Rogaland (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15). Trap: Do not use the present-needs sentence as the definition.

June 2025, Question 2

Question: In what way can a regional innovation system promote or inhibit industrial restructuring? Use examples. Thesis: A regional system promotes restructuring through variety and collective work, and inhibits it when specialised. Moves: 1. Read both sides of the command [Class 9, slide 39] 2. Name the four trajectories (Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 328) 3. Show how variety can promote (Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 330) 4. Show how lock-in can inhibit (Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 328) 5. Use the two regions, then close (Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15) Example: Maritime batteries are embedded, and a cluster plus procurement built the market (Njøs et al. 2020, p. 281). Trap: Keep exhaustion in the four names. Upgrading means renewal.

June 2025, Question 3

Question: How can technological innovation systems be guided to promote sustainable transformation? Use examples. Thesis: Guide the system by rating functions, aiming policy at the weak ones, and choosing the direction. Moves: 1. Read the demand for levers [Class 9, slide 39] 2. Define the seven functions (Hekkert et al. 2007, p. 421) 3. Aim policy at the weak ones (Steen et al. 2024, p. 9) 4. Choose the direction on purpose (Schlaile et al. 2017, p. 4) 5. Use capture and batteries, then close (Steen et al. 2024, p. 4) Example: Longship adds two capture sites, ship transport and offshore storage (Steen et al. 2024, p. 4). Trap: Do not call knowledge and legitimation strengths of the capture system.
