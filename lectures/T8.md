## 2. Innovation as systemic
<!-- slides: 5, 6, 7, 8 -->
<!-- readings: none new -->

Slide 5 quotes two definitions of innovation systems by Markard and Truffer, from 2008. First, they are networks of actors and institutions that develop, spread and use innovations. Second, they are a set of organisations and institutions and the relationships between them.

The slide then explains each part:
Organisations are the actors.
Institutions are formal laws and rules, and informal norms, perceptions and routines for ways of doing.
The relationships between organisations and institutions are the links and networks.

Slide 6 lists the system perspectives of the course:
Territorial innovation systems, at national, regional and global level, put geographical embedding at the centre.
Entrepreneurial ecosystems put the entrepreneur and the entrepreneur's context at the centre.
Technological innovation systems put technologies and policy instruments at the centre.

Slide 7 says that these perspectives understand a system differently, but share some ideas. All see innovation as interactive. A system structures innovation practice, which is what path dependence means. Yet systems also contain agency: organisations and actors who act. So there is room to change a system, through path renewal or by setting up new paths. One example is institutional work that changes relationships and meaning-making, to stimulate sustainable transformation.

Slide 8 repeats that territorial innovation systems focus on geographical embedding.

## 3. National, regional and global innovation systems
<!-- slides: 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 -->
<!-- readings: Fagerberg et al. 2009, pp. 437, 438 -->

Slide 9 recaps national innovation systems from Lecture 1. Each nation-state has its own formal and informal institutions, its own R&D profile, natural resources and industrial history. Together these give countries different capacity to innovate.

Slide 10 returns to the Norwegian paradox. Norway has high productivity and value creation, but relatively low R&D intensity. The slide quotes Fagerberg and colleagues, from 2009, on pages 437 and 438. They see an important strength of the Norwegian system in its strong performance in knowledge diffusion and cooperation in innovation. The lecture explains this by an economy based on natural resources and shaped by concession and industrial policies. In the oil and gas industry, specialised knowledge, skills and suppliers innovated through interactive processes. These combine science-based learning, called STI, with learning by doing, using and interacting, called DUI.

Slide 11 moves to regional innovation systems from Lecture 2. A regional innovation system is the infrastructure, network dynamics and institutional conditions that hinder or promote innovation in a region. Hard factors include natural resources, specialised labour, suppliers and knowledge organisations. Soft factors include networks, institutions, tacit knowledge and local buzz.

Slides 12 and 13 describe the three subsystems of a regional innovation system. Slide 13 cites Tödtling and Trippl, from 2005, and Isaksen and Trippl, from 2017.
First, the production subsystem consists of firms and industries, often organised in clusters.
Second, the knowledge subsystem consists of research and education organisations.
Third, the support subsystem includes cluster organisations, incubators and political governance bodies.
Knowledge flows and learning go on within and between the subsystems. Formal and informal rules stimulate or hinder innovation.

Slide 14 looks at the production subsystem. A cluster, in the sense of Michael Porter, brings together similar firms, specialised suppliers, service providers and related industries. The slide states that firms in regional clusters are more innovative than firms outside them. Clusters involve shared tacit knowledge, competition, collaboration and workers moving between firms.

Slide 15 turns to the knowledge subsystem. Universities have three missions: education, research, and contributing to economic and social development. Today, rather than assuming research helps automatically, the focus is on how universities work with the world around them.

Slide 16 gives an example of the support subsystem: the Section for Innovation Studies at HVL, where the lecturer works. It researches how innovation happens and how research can give it a desired direction. Its projects include RRI2scale with Vestland County Council, the hydrogen research centre HyValue, and AQUABALANCE with the fish-farming industry.

Slide 17 addresses global innovation systems. Globalisation means studying global innovation networks that link national and regional systems. The key system elements are knowledge, new markets, capital and legitimacy. Parts of these elements are found in different places. Structural coupling is about how far, and in what way, the subsystems are linked.

## 4. Entrepreneurial ecosystems and technological systems
<!-- slides: 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25 -->
<!-- readings: Njøs et al. 2020, pp. 266, 268 -->
<!-- sourced: 2018 | knowledge/PAPER_INDEX.md, reading 6: Spigel & Harrison (2018) -->

Slide 18 recaps entrepreneurial ecosystems from Lecture 4. An entrepreneurial ecosystem is a framework that links entrepreneurship to networks whose dynamics matter for entrepreneurial activity. The framework stresses communities of practice.

Slide 19 shows entrepreneurs at the centre of overlapping contexts: organisational, institutional and policy, social, and industry and technology. Slide 20 says that the explanation runs from agency to structure. The most important factors come from the entrepreneurs themselves, through knowledge sharing and learning in their community of practice. An ecosystem must grow from the bottom up; nobody can create one by decision. Politicians can support the entrepreneurial community. But without leadership and commitment from that community, measures have limited effect.

Slide 21 turns to technological innovation systems, Lecture 6. The slide defines a technological innovation system, or TIS, as a network of actors in a bounded technology field. They interact within a specific institutional infrastructure, to create, spread and use a technology. The slide names three elements: actors, networks, and institutions such as laws, rules and norms. It then quotes van Alphen and colleagues, from 2011. Actors range from individuals and firms to universities and public agencies, and institutions include laws, policy targets and social norms.

Slide 22 links back to Lecture 1, repeating its figure of teams, tools and tasks. Technology cannot be understood in isolation from the social system it is part of. Slide 23 quotes Hanson and colleagues, from 2011. Technology development is a process of including firms, knowledge organisations, financial institutions, public agencies and others in developing, producing and using the new technology.

Slide 24 opens by saying that technical change alone is not enough. Socio-cultural and political conditions help decide which changes start, and whether they are implemented. The slide then lists the seven functions of a TIS: entrepreneurial activity, knowledge development, knowledge diffusion through networks, guidance of the search, market formation, resource mobilisation and legitimacy. The approach combines innovation system research with transition studies, and looks for drivers and barriers for new green technology. The slide names two examples: carbon capture and storage, and maritime battery technology.

These are the two cases in this lecture's reading, by Njøs and colleagues, from 2020. On page 266, the authors argue that neither territorial dynamics nor technological characteristics alone explain path creation. What matters is how the two connect. On page 268, they adopt a definition by MacKinnon and colleagues. Path creation is the emergence of new development trajectories in a region, based on the growth of new industrial sectors, products, techniques or forms of organisation.

Slide 25 sums up the course in a table of focus, example and readings.
Entrepreneurial ecosystems focus on conditions for entrepreneurship, on agency, and on the founder community. The example is UiB and Marineholmen. The reading is Spigel and Harrison, which the slide dates 2017, though the article on your reading list is from 2018.
National innovation systems focus on national conditions, where knowledge policy and industrial policy are central. The example is Norway, with Fagerberg and colleagues.
Regional innovation systems focus on the three regional subsystems. The example is Hordaland and Rogaland, with Fløysand and colleagues, from 2022, in Lectures 2 and 3.
Technological innovation systems focus on the transition to a new technology, as a function of the seven functions. The column also names the multi-level perspective. The examples are carbon capture and storage, electric cars, wind power and hydrogen. The readings are Njøs and colleagues, and Hekkert and colleagues, from 2007.

## 5. Sustainable transformation and institutional work
<!-- slides: 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 -->
<!-- readings: Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 17 -->

Slide 26 notes that the system perspectives in the course have paid less attention to sustainable transformation. One question is how systems can contribute to green industrial restructuring. On page 17, Fløysand and colleagues, from 2022, define it with a phrase from UNEP. It means processes of change that reduce carbon emissions and pollution, enhance energy and resource efficiency, and prevent the loss of biodiversity and ecosystem services.

The slide also compares the state's role. In entrepreneurial ecosystems, it coordinates rather than steers. In territorial systems, it actively supports knowledge development, the regional subsystems and internationalisation. In technological systems, policy at all levels must remove obstacles to the desired transition.

Slide 27 presents Schlaile and colleagues. The slide dates their article 2019, but the article on your reading list is from 2017. They argue that supporting innovation in itself is not enough. Innovation systems need a direction to solve the challenges of society. A quote on the slide calls for more research on directionality, legitimacy, responsibility, and how these relate. The slide explains the word normative with a dictionary definition: it sets how something ought to be. Separately, it says that technical solutions alone cannot reach sustainability goals. Economic, institutional, cultural and organisational conditions must change too.

Slide 28 is about direction. Defining targeted missions for society is a political act, as Janssen and colleagues noted in 2021. The slide asks: who wins and who loses from the innovations? Innovations can lead to conflict, power struggles and loss of biodiversity. So the slide calls for a balance: broad involvement, but with a clear direction and concrete goals.

Slide 29 is about transformative innovation policy. It gives three definitions from the UN, from 2023. Economic sustainability is the efficient use and fair distribution of resources. Social sustainability is the well-being, equity and inclusion of communities. Environmental sustainability means using nature without harming it beyond its capacity to regenerate. They matter: Question 1 of the June 2025 exam asked what you understand by sustainability.

Slides 30 and 31 return to institutional work in Hordaland and Rogaland, from Lecture 3. Slide 30 quotes Lawrence and colleagues, from 2011. Institutional work is physical and mental effort aimed at affecting an institution or a set of institutions. It can maintain, change or create institutions, both formal rules and informal ways of doing. The slide borrows a phrase from North, from 1990, to place these in social, political and economic relations.

Slide 31 makes three points. First, institutional work both shapes the region and its innovation system, called change agency, and is shaped by them, called reproductive agency. Second, institutional work in both regions aims at a green shift. Yet, somewhat unintentionally, oil and gas still dominates business in the regions. Third, the slide asks for alternative explanations. Could the work that prolongs oil and gas come from national key actors, such as Equinor and the national authorities, rather than from the regions? Or could it come from institutional work in global innovation systems? The slide leaves these as open questions.

Slide 32 shows the trinity of innovation from Lecture 1: the triangle of teams, tools and tasks. It comes from the 2013 conference paper by Fløysand and colleagues, which we only describe in our own words. Sustainable transformation means that technologies must solve environmental and distributional tasks in addition to economic and productive ones. An arrow marks the triangle as the necessary relations of an innovation. Below it, a box says that direction depends on how institutional conditions, political conditions and knowledge interact in networks. A second arrow marks these as contingent relations.

## 6. Preparing for the exam
<!-- slides: 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41 -->
<!-- readings: none new -->
<!-- sourced: 2026 | Kursplan INN514 - 2026.pptx, the course plan's year -->

Slide 33 sets out the exam. It is a written school exam. You answer Question 1, and then either Question 2 or Question 3. Arrive well rested, and plan your time, because four hours go fast. The slide gives the date as 27 November, in the year the slides were made. The 2026 course plan lists the exam without a date, so check it when it is published. Both past exam papers, from December 2024 and June 2025, add two facts. No aids are allowed, and the two questions you answer count equally.

Slides 33 to 35 show that Question 1 tests your understanding of concepts: what do you understand by this term? Slide 34 gives examples: institutions, agency and sustainable transformation. It asks how these are defined and used in the readings and lectures. Slide 35 says to start with a direct definition, such as: "By sustainable transformation, I understand …". Question 1 of the December 2024 exam asked exactly this: what do you understand by innovation system, sustainable transformation, agency and institutions?

Slide 36 asks the class: do you have suggestions for exam questions? Try it yourself: write down one question you would set.

Slides 37 and 38 give the lecturer's two example questions. The first asks: what do you understand by a technological innovation system, and how can it be linked to the seven functions? The second asks: "How can innovation systems promote or inhibit sustainable transformation? Use examples." Notice the words "promote or inhibit". The December 2024 exam used them in Question 2, about agency, and in Question 3, about technologies. The June 2025 exam used them in Question 2, about a regional innovation system and industrial restructuring. So practise answers that show both sides.

Slides 39 to 41 show how to structure your answer.
In the introduction, slide 39 says, present how you understand the question and how you have chosen to answer it. For example: "To discuss how …, I will first …, and then use examples to show that …". You may also introduce one or more main arguments.
In the main part, slide 40 says, start from relevant perspectives in the readings, lectures and seminars, and share your reflections. Use paragraphs, and perhaps subheadings. Start by clarifying concepts, put the readings to use, and bring in one or more examples.
In the conclusion, slide 41 says, sum up what you discussed and what the discussion showed. Make sure you answer the questions the task raises. If you made claims in the introduction, you can return to them here.

## 7. Marking guide and course evaluation
<!-- slides: 42, 43 -->
<!-- readings: none new -->

Slide 42 is about the course evaluation. You receive an email with a link to a digital form, and the lecturer asks you to answer it.

Slide 43 shows the guidance for examiners in INN514. Read it as a checklist.

For the course's focus, readings and formal rules, examiners look at Canvas, the slides from this summary lecture and the general grade descriptions.

For the concept clarifications, which Question 1 asks for, examiners check whether you know the central concepts and understand them in an academically informed way.

For the discussion question, Question 2 or 3, the slide lists six criteria.
Structure: the weight given to the introduction, theory, empirical material and analysis, and the conclusion.
The problem statement: follow up the question you were given, for example by breaking it into sub-questions that show how you interpret the task.
Language and argument: your writing should show independence and that you understand the theory and concepts you use. Build an argument and follow it through every part, including the conclusion. Keep the presentation tidy.
Theory: here the examiners ask questions. Are the readings put to use? Are the theories relevant, understood and well presented? Does the theory fit the problem and the empirical material? Can you link problem, theory and empirical material?
Analysis: can you find and use relevant empirical material, such as good cases, and relate it to theory? Examiners look for answers that are analytical and critical.
Conclusion: can you return to the problem statement and sum up the important findings, linking back to theory and empirical material? Can you reflect on strengths and weaknesses, or on the limits of your discussion?

These criteria follow the structure from chapter 6, so an answer built that way also follows the marking guide.
