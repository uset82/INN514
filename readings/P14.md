# Reading 14: Change agency and reproductive agency in the course of industrial path evolution
<!-- reading: P14 | authors: Bækkelund | year: 2021 | source: Regional Studies 55(4) | pdf: 14 Bækkelund 2021.pdf | pages: cover, 757-768 | lectures: T5 | agent: WorkBuddy, revised by Claude Code -->

## 1. The paper in brief
<!-- pages: all -->

Research on regional industry paths has mostly looked at agency in path creation, so radical change agency has moved to the centre. Bækkelund argues that reproductive agency remains under-conceptualized. It is often read as a source of lock-in or obstruction, while its other roles have scarcely been studied (page 757). The paper therefore introduces a distinction between change agency and reproductive agency, and follows both across the whole life of a path. Its research question is: how do change and reproductive agency vary in the course of path evolution?

Two definitions carry the argument. An industry path is a distinct type of economic activity that gathers enough critical mass to produce self-reinforcing effects, a definition the author takes from Fredin and colleagues on page 758. Agency, in the words of Sotarauta and Suvinen, is action or intervention by an actor to produce a particular effect. From Grillitsch and Sotarauta the paper takes three types of change agency: Schumpeterian entrepreneurship, institutional entrepreneurship and place-based leadership. It then builds a counter-category for each. Replicative entrepreneurship, institutional work and maintenance leadership form the reproductive end of a range, with change leadership on the change side (page 759). The framework follows Martin's path-as-process model and is summarised in Figure 1 on page 760.

The evidence comes from tourism in three villages in Western Norway: Flåm, Odda and Sogndal. They share formal institutions and landscape, as all three lie at the end of a fjord branch, yet their tourism sectors developed very differently. The design therefore follows the logic of most similar cases with different outcomes. Bækkelund traced each path backwards from the present, using interviews and secondary sources in two rounds. There are 29 interviews in all, including nine background interviews and three follow-ups in August 2020 (page 761).

In all three cases, reproductive agency is stronger in the preformation phase and in path development, and change agency is stronger in path creation (page 765). The shift into path creation can often be traced to a critical juncture, which in two of the cases was triggered by a problematic situation. The author adds that a critical juncture also needs an opportunity space. Local actors were key in starting each new path, while non-local actors contributed resources not available locally. Flåm shows that public actors can enact Schumpeterian entrepreneurship, and Sogndal shows one person's initiative turning place-based leadership from maintenance towards change. The cases also differ: Flåm has shown more change agency than the other two.

The paper's contribution is to give reproductive agency its own categories. It concludes that change agency is key to path creation, while reproductive agency is necessary for the path to develop. New paths cannot be explained by local agency or exogenous shocks alone, but by a mix of local and exogenous developments (page 766). The author names the limits too. All three cases come from rural tourism, which has peculiarities of its own, so the argument should be investigated in other regions and industries. The multiscalarity of agency also needs to be explored beyond the local and non-local distinction.

The paper is a core reading for Lecture 5, Agency and innovation systems in change. Its distinction between change and reproductive agency, and between local and non-local actors, gives you precise terms for Question 1 of the December 2024 exam, which asks what you understand by agency. Question 2 of that exam asks how agency in innovation systems can promote or inhibit sustainable transformation. Here is an example of our own, not from the paper: the framework lets you ask which actors push a green path forward and which keep an old path going. The paper's nearest link is Flåm, where local actors invest in green technology while working to shape new environmental rules.

## 2. Why the paper adds a second kind of agency
<!-- pages: cover, 757-759 -->

### Cover
<!-- kind: cover -->
The publisher's cover sheet gives the citation: Bækkelund, Regional Studies 55(4), pages 757 to 768, published online on 15 March 2021.

### Page 757
The article opens by naming its problem. Evolutionary economic geography has turned to agency, but mostly to change agency in path creation and diversification. Reproductive agency is under-conceptualized and often read as lock-in or obstruction. Bækkelund asks how change and reproductive agency vary in the course of path evolution. The cases are tourism in Flåm, Odda and Sogndal, villages by the Sognefjord and the Hardangerfjord. Tourism suits the question because it is networked and involves many different actors. The abstract states the finding: both kinds of agency matter for industry path development.

### Page 758
The theoretical section starts from path dependence. Garud and Karnøe first challenged its focus on structure with the idea of path creation, and Martin made the model processual. The paper defines an industry path as a distinct economic activity that reaches enough critical mass to become self-reinforcing, following Fredin and colleagues. Agency, borrowed from Sotarauta and Suvinen, is action by an actor to produce a particular effect. Change usually comes from several actors, and an actor's network is part of its agency. From Grillitsch and Sotarauta comes the trinity of change agency: Schumpeterian entrepreneurship, institutional entrepreneurship and place-based leadership.

### Page 759
Here the paper builds the reproductive half of the model. Replicative entrepreneurship, from Baumol, covers the many new firms that resemble existing ones. Institutional work, following Lawrence and colleagues, includes efforts that maintain existing institutions. From place-based leadership the paper derives a pair: change leadership and maintenance leadership. Together these form a range from change agency to reproductive agency. The paper adds a distinction between local and non-local actors, since non-local actors may hold placeless power. In the preformation phase, reproductive agency prevails, but it can also build the networks and capabilities that later change needs.

## 3. Phases of a path, and how they were traced
<!-- pages: 760-762 -->

### Page 760
The paper defines a critical juncture with Capoccia and Kelemen: a period in which agents' choices are much more likely to affect the outcome. A juncture needs an opportunity space and events that call for agency, such as a problematic situation that pushes actors out of routine. Path creation requires Schumpeterian entrepreneurship combined with wider change. The move into path development is gradual, because those who created the path gain vested interests. Figure 1 sums up the model: preformation, a critical juncture, path creation, then path development, which leads either to a stable state or to a dynamic path.

### Page 761
Too much reproductive agency is likely to lead to lock-in, while a better mix with change agency keeps a path dynamic. The method section explains how each path was traced backwards from the present, using interviews and secondary sources in two rounds. There are 29 interviews, including nine background interviews and three follow-ups in August 2020. The villages share institutions and landscape but saw very different tourism, so the design compares most similar cases with different outcomes. Sogndal has about 4000 inhabitants, Odda about 7000, and Flåm about 400 inhabitants but about a million visitors a year.

### Page 762
Figure 2 maps the three villages and also marks Bergen. In Sogndal, new football stadium requirements led to a stadium on the campus plot. This began a new era of local collaboration and maintenance leadership, which some informants call the Sogndal model. In 2008 the same networks backed a mountain sport festival, and one person's initiative turned place-based leadership from maintenance towards change. The festival built the valley's reputation for snow. Applicant numbers to the university college then grew faster than the national average, and one board member does not think that was a coincidence.

## 4. Three villages, three tourism paths
<!-- pages: 763-765 -->

### Page 763
Odda was once Norway's top tourist destination and became an industrial town after its first smelting plant opened in 1908. The smelter closed in 2003 with the loss of 200 jobs, a critical juncture, but a dispute over the old plant held back a response. The new path came from a hotel co-owner and the regional marketing organization, who both picked the Trolltunga rock formation. Visitors grew from 1000 in 2009 to 50,000 in 2015. Growth pains followed, with 40 mountain rescues in 2016, and the shared solutions show change agency helping to reproduce a path.

### Page 764
In Odda, replicative entrepreneurship followed, and visitor numbers stabilized by 2019. Flåm's tourism rests on a railway built in 1941, but its path starts with the risk of closure. In 1997 the state railway decided to end operations. The mayor and the tourism secretary answered with a tourism business model. The railway joined on condition that the municipality buy the infrastructure and build a cruise terminal, which hydroelectric income made possible. The deal combined institutional and Schumpeterian entrepreneurship. Replicative entrepreneurship marked the development phase, and in 2018 Parliament decided that the world heritage fjords must be zero emission by 2026.

### Page 765
The discussion draws the three cases together. The shift from reproductive to change agency can often be traced to a critical juncture, and in two of the cases a problematic situation triggered it. Local actors were key in starting each new path, while non-local actors supplied resources not available locally. Public actors can also enact Schumpeterian entrepreneurship, as Flåm shows, and one actor may take several roles. The move back towards reproductive agency is gradual, because those who built the path gain vested interests. Flåm has shown more change agency than the other two cases.

## 5. Conclusions and the reference list
<!-- pages: 766-768 -->

### Page 766
The conclusions look ahead and sum up. In Flåm, local actors invest in green technology while doing institutional work to shape the new environmental rules, so the path seems to continue as a dynamic process. The paper states its contribution: change agency is key to path creation, and reproductive agency is necessary for the path to develop. New paths come from a mix of local agency and exogenous developments, not from either alone. The limits are that all the cases come from rural tourism, and that the multiscalarity of agency needs more work.
