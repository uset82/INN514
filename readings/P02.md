## 1. The paper in brief
<!-- pages: all -->

This paper investigates how innovation can be conceptualized as an interplay between discursive ideas, material objects and practical social action. The authors address the tension between generic models and context-specific studies in innovation research. They ask whether one analytical model can capture both what all innovation processes share and what is specific to each context in which innovation happens. This question matters because previous research shifted from measuring physical patents and products toward analyzing social networks and institutions, pushing materiality into the background.

The paper relies on several key concepts. On page 2 and page 9, the authors draw on actor-network theory from Latour, adopting the concept of actants to grant agency to non-human entities such as technologies, tools and natural objects alongside human individuals and organizations. On page 3 and page 8, they build on critical realism from Sayer, separating abstract necessary relations from place-specific contingent relations. On page 9, they borrow the trinity concept from Schwarz and Thompson, who defined technological development as patterns of people organizing, patterns of material things and patterns of ideas. On page 10, they operationalize this as the pragmatic innovation complex, where companies as actors use tools as objects to solve tasks as ideas. On page 11, they use the cultural economy concept of Amin and Thrift to show how contingent regulations, history and localization shape these relations.

The paper uses a conceptual and methodological approach. The authors combine the epistemology of critical realism with actor-network theory and review historical shifts in innovation theory, from early diffusion models to regional innovation systems. To illustrate their framework, they analyze the governing imperatives of commercial innovation within capitalist market economies.

The authors argue that innovation is neither purely material nor purely discursive. Instead, innovation emerges through practical interplay within heterogeneous networks of actants. In capitalist economies, profit maximization and productivity act as necessary relations. However, other interests also count. Shifting alliances of stakeholders, such as public authorities and environmental organizations, can influence innovation from case to case. For some of them, reducing carbon dioxide emissions or contributing to green growth may matter, and outcomes can be decided where public and market interests meet.

The paper contributes an analytical model that bridges nomothetic generalization and ideographic context. As for limits, the authors state that they do not intend to conduct a normative evaluation of innovation functionality. They also emphasize that interactions between humans, technologies and ideas cannot be investigated through necessary relationships alone, requiring qualitative investigation of contingent conditions.

This reading belongs to Lecture 1: Innovation as systemic and sustainable transformation. In the course, it provides the teams, tools and tasks model that explains how innovation operates as a practical system. In our own reading, the distinction between necessary and contingent relations can help with Question 1 of the December 2024 exam, which asks what you understand by innovation system, sustainable transformation, agency and institutions. It can also help with Question 1 of the June 2025 exam, which asks what you understand by a regional innovation system, institutional work and sustainability. Here is an example of our own, not from the paper: a solar energy project requires hardware components, engineering teams and policy goals to align, but local planning regulations and community priorities decide whether the installation proceeds. The paper's nearest point, on page 13, is that reducing carbon dioxide emissions or contributing to green growth may matter to public authorities and environmental organizations.

## 2. From materiality to idea and practice
<!-- pages: 1-5 -->

### Page 1
This page introduces a conference paper presented at the 5th Nordic Geographers Meeting held in Iceland in June 2013. The authors, Arnt Fløysand, Stig-Erik Jakobsen and Knut Hidle, represent the University of Bergen and Bergen University College. The paper examines the trinity of innovation, exploring relationships between actors, ideas and objects in innovation practice. The authors explicitly note that this draft is a paper in preparation and should not be quoted.

### Page 2
Page 2 presents the research problem by exploring innovation as a discursive, material and practical phenomenon. The authors highlight a paradox in innovation studies between context-specific uniqueness and generic abstractions. To bridge ideographic and nomothetic approaches, they propose an analytical model that combines generic innovation properties with contextual specificities. Their epistemological framework builds on actor-network theory from Latour, which highlights interplays between ideas, materiality and practice, combined with realism from Sayer, which distinguishes between necessary and contingent relations.

### Page 3
Page 3 establishes the theoretical foundation by integrating critical realism and actor-network theory. From Sayer, the authors adopt the distinction between abstract necessary relations and contingent, place-specific structures. From actor-network theory, they introduce the concept of actants to examine the social, material and immaterial dimensions of innovation. Reviewing the history of innovation, the authors note that the term is positively charged in Western culture, often linked to progress and breaking established paradigms, as discussed by Kuhn, Foucault and Shils.

### Page 4
Page 4 traces the history and roots of the word. Godin shows that Plato and Aristotle valued imitation, which was seen as positive until the mid-eighteenth century. Godin also points to Nelson and Winter, who link a firm's innovation to its routines, and to Levitt, for whom imitation is often a survival strategy. The Latin root means to renew, or to change into something new. The authors conclude that the word's original meaning already refers to materiality, idea and practice. Early research, by contrast, counted patents and new products.

### Page 5
Page 5 evaluates Hägerstrand's spatial diffusion model, noting that it demonstrated distance delays in information flows among farmers adopting cattle vaccination. However, early diffusion research treated innovation as an objective, measurable entity while neglecting ideas. Later scholars recognized innovation as a sociocultural process. Citing Polanyi, the authors note that the economy operates as an institutional process. Nelson and Winter incorporated organizational learning into evolutionary theory. Finally, systems of innovation perspectives emerged from Freeman, Nelson and Lundvall, emphasizing interactive learning among diverse actors.

## 3. Systems, relational perspectives and actants
<!-- pages: 6-9 -->

### Page 6
Page 6 examines the systems-of-innovation perspective developed by Lundvall and Cooke. Lundvall emphasized that innovation systems have open, situation-specific boundaries and generate unexpected outcomes. Policy makers embraced this framework because it provided a proactive role for governance and network steering, as analyzed by Kooiman and Klijn and Edelenbos. Putnam and colleagues highlighted social capital and trust as essential foundations for innovative socioeconomic systems. However, popular management manuals often detached these insights from local context, moving materiality to the background while focusing primarily on discourse.

### Page 7
Page 7 traces the shift, in geography, from the distribution of innovation to place, and Bathelt and Glückler argue for the relational perspective: innovations grow from socially intertwined systems, and practice shapes a system in ways we cannot foresee. Right relations between industrial actors, research institutions and political functions are necessary but not sufficient, and practice can lock a system in. What counts is how actors practise relationships: values, role expectations and informal learning. The page closes by saying innovation escapes deconstruction and that relational dynamics are usually unpacked only implicitly.

### Page 8
Page 8 synthesizes the core critique of existing innovation literature. While relational approaches represent progress, they still neglect how material things, ideas and social practice work together in innovation. To resolve this neglect, the authors turn to two epistemological frameworks. Actor-network theory from Latour restores material objects to the analysis of action. Critical realism, cited through Johnston and colleagues, separates necessary causal relations from contingent local configurations. The authors propose combining these insights into an integrated analytical model.

### Page 9
Page 9 details the actor-network concept of actants, citing Latour. Following Latour, an actant is any entity that acts, or that others treat as acting: people and organizations, but also nature, art, technology and tools. In critical realism, Johnston and colleagues define realism through necessary causal powers realized under contingent conditions. To build their trinity model, the authors draw on Schwarz and Thompson, who defined technology development through patterns of people organizing, patterns of material things, and patterns of ideas or cultural biases.

## 4. The pragmatic innovation complex and analytical model
<!-- pages: 10-13 -->

### Page 10
Page 10 outlines the pragmatic innovation complex illustrated in Figure 1. The authors define actors as technology producers, users, entrepreneurs and authorities; objects as landscapes, technology and tools; and ideas as knowledge, narratives and norms. In Figure 1, three elements form a practical triangle: company as actor, tools as objects, and tasks as ideas. Technique links company and tools; production links tools and tasks, including unintended outputs such as carbon dioxide emissions; and rationale links company and tasks through profit-maximizing goals in capitalist economies.

### Page 11
Page 11 argues that the simple triangle is not enough on its own to understand wider innovation practice. Citing Latour, the authors observe that innovation lives in meetings, plans, regulations and experiments, mixed with other social agencies. Critical realism resolves this tension: the actor, object and idea relationships are the necessary properties of innovation, while contingent relationships give them their content. The contingent ones are cultural economy, history, governance and localization. Following Amin and Thrift, a cultural economy holds actors, objects and ideas together through rules, shared conventions, technologies and ways of working.

### Page 12
Page 12 discusses Figure 2, which diagrams the governing imperative of innovation alongside necessary and contingent relations. In Figure 2, objects, actors and ideas form necessary relations, while cultural economy, history, governance and territorial localization form contingent relations. In capitalist economies, profit maximization and productivity serve as necessary governing imperatives. However, citing Polanyi, the authors note that anthropology reveals alternative economic arrangements based on delayed exchange. Sometimes, and perhaps more importantly for outcomes, different complexes of actors, objects and ideas reflect differing interests.

### Page 13
Page 13 examines how stakeholder alliances shape innovation outcomes. Beyond traditional corporate stakeholders such as shareholders, customers, employees and suppliers, innovation involves public authorities, universities, local communities and non-governmental organizations. For public authorities and environmental groups, innovation can target sustainability challenges by reducing carbon dioxide emissions and promoting green growth. Dominant narratives determine what society considers beneficial innovation practice. Citing necessary and contingent relationships, the authors conclude that qualitative methods such as participatory observation and thick description are essential to study innovation practice.

## 5. Conclusions and the reference list
<!-- pages: 14-17 -->

### Page 14
Page 14 concludes that innovation has discursive, material and practical dimensions. Innovation studies have moved from counting patents and products to analysing learning in regional innovation systems. That shift is welcome, the authors say, but the material foundation of innovation must not be forgotten. They combine the actant of actor-network theory with critical realism's split between necessary and contingent relations, so that innovation appears as firms using tools to solve tasks. Cultural economy, history, governance and local territory shape that practice.

### Page 15
The conclusion ends with what the model offers. It lets researchers treat innovation as arising from a system that links ideas, practice and materiality. This can be done, the authors add, without ignoring the cultural, economic and political settings that innovations belong to. With that, the paper's argument is complete, and the reference list begins further down the same page.
