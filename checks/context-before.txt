Chapter: Class 4, chapter 3: Two models of the ecosystem. Nora Geirsdotter Bækkelund. Slides 11–13.
---
To turn the broad concept of an ecosystem into a practical framework, researchers have developed analytical models. The lecture introduces two influential examples.

The first model comes from Stam and van de Ven in 2021, shown on slide 11. On page 810, they build on van de Ven's work from 1993 on the infrastructure for entrepreneurship. On page 813, they present a framework divided into two main layers.

At the base sit institutional arrangements. These are formal institutions, such as legal rules and property rights; informal culture, such as social attitudes towards ambition; and networks linking local actors. Resting on these institutions are resource endowments. These include physical infrastructure, market demand, intermediaries who connect people, talent, scientific knowledge, leadership, and access to finance.

The model links these parts through three propositions, set out on page 814. Proposition one states that the elements are mutually interdependent and co-evolve. Proposition two, which the authors call upward causation, states that the elements together explain the level of productive entrepreneurship in a territory, the ecosystem's output. Productive entrepreneurship means activity that creates net economic and social value. Proposition three, downward causation, describes a feedback loop: successful entrepreneurial outputs feed back into the ecosystem, enriching local institutions and resources over time.

The second model, presented on slide 12, comes from Spigel and Harrison. The slide also cites a 2016 paper by Stam and Spigel. The model sorts the ecosystem into three interdependent elements: cultural, social and material.

Cultural elements provide the shared outlook. They include positive social attitudes towards business creation, inspiring local success stories, and an acceptance of failure as a natural step in learning.

Social elements provide the human connections and resources. They include risk capital, skilled workers, and experienced mentors who advise young founders.

Material elements provide the physical and organizational backbone. They include startup incubators and accelerators, university research centres, transport infrastructure, and targeted public policies.

Slide 13 adds a short video titled How to Build a Thriving Entrepreneurial Ecosystem Anywhere. On page 152 of their article, Spigel and Harrison explain that the global popularity of ecosystems was accelerated by practitioners. In particular, venture investor Brad Feld wrote about startup communities in Boulder, Colorado, while Daniel Isenberg wrote about ecosystem domains. Both argued that a flourishing startup scene depends on community cooperation rather than bureaucratic planning.

- Paper 1: Fagerberg, Mowery & Verspagen (2009), "The evolution of Norway's national innovation system", Science and Public Policy 36(6). For class 1. In brief: This paper studies how science, technology and innovation policy evolved together with the industrial structure of Norway, a small, open, resource-based economy. Norway combines high growth in productivity and income with comparatively low investment in research and development. The authors ask how that is possible. Previous work on national innovation systems, they note, gave little attention to institutions and politics, perhaps because it often examined snapshots at one point in time. - Paper 2: Fløysand, Jakobsen & Hidle (2013), "The “trinity” of innovation: actors, ideas and objects in innovation practice", Conference paper, 5th Nordic Geographers' Meeting, Reykjavík (paper in preparation, do not quote). For class 1. In brief: This paper investigates how innovation can be conceptualized as an interplay between discursive ideas, material objects and practical social action. The authors address the tension between generic models and context-specific studies in innovation research. They ask whether one analytical model can capture both what all innovation processes share and what is specific to each context in which innovation happens. This question matters because previous research shifted from measuring physical patents and products toward analyzing social networks and institutions, pushing materiality into the background. Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 From materiality to idea and practice; 3 Systems, relational perspectives and actants; 4 The pragmatic innovation complex and analytical model; 5 Conclusions and the reference list.

For class 2 and class 3. In brief: This paper investigates how regional key actors use institutional work to enable or constrain green industrial restructuring in petroleum-dependent regional innovation systems. The cases are Hordaland and Rogaland, two adjacent regions in Western Norway (page 15). While evolutionary economic geography has studied agency in regional path development, it struggles to explain recursive dynamics between actors and institutions. Changing strong path dependency requires altering institutions, but regional trajectories carry cemented institutional logics that resist change. Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 Introduction, regional innovation systems and institutional work; 3 Materials, methods and regional innovation system structures; 4 Institutional work, cluster dynamics and green restructuring; 5 Conclusions and the reference list.

For class 1 and class 2 and class 9. In brief: The paper asks how the innovation systems framework can support transformations towards sustainability (page 1). Humanity faces multiple crises, and Rockström and colleagues claim that several planetary boundaries have already been crossed. Such wicked problems have emergent, socially embedded causes and uncertain effects. Technological solutions alone will not suffice, the authors argue; wicked problems call for transformations in economic, institutional, technical, cultural and organizational dimensions (page 2). Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 Introduction and the limits of conventional innovation systems; 3 The normative dimension and dedicated innovation systems; 4 Responsibility, building blocks and the research agenda; 5 Conclusions and the reference list.

For class 2. In brief: This book chapter examines business development and regional renewal in Western Norway from an evolutionary economic perspective. The authors analyze how industrial restructuring operates across geographical scales, from regional systems to individual firms and entrepreneurs (page 323). They argue that internationalisation and related variety decide how far a region renews, and ask whether such conditions can be traced in Western Norway (pages 323, 324). Western Norway encompasses the four counties of Rogaland, Hordaland, Sogn and Fjordane, and Møre and Romsdal. Its economy concentrates ocean industries: petroleum, maritime transport and seafood. Falling oil prices in 2015 and 2016 made the question urgent (page 324). Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 Concepts and theories of regional renewal; 3 Industrial paths, related variety and regional structure; 4 Cluster programmes, path creation and related variety; 5 Pipelines, entrepreneurship and cross-industry innovation; 6 Conclusions and the reference list.

In brief: Spigel and Harrison ask what an entrepreneurial ecosystem actually is, and whether the idea deserves more than its status as a policy slogan. On page 152 they set out the problem. The term became popular faster than research could follow, so it has turned into what they call, following Sayer, a chaotic conception: something that describes rather than explains. In brief: The paper asks a question the ecosystem literature had left hanging: if regional context shapes entrepreneurship, how should that context be defined and measured, and does its quality show up in entrepreneurial output? Their answer has three parts: a definition, a ten-element model, and an index applied to the twelve Dutch provinces. Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 The concept, the definition and the model; 3 The ten elements and the three propositions; 4 Measuring the elements and the output; 5 The correlation table and the two figures; 6 The index, the results and the discussion; 7 Limitations and the reference list.
- Paper 8: Autio et al. (2014), "Entrepreneurial innovation: the importance of context", Research Policy 43(7). For class 4. In brief: Autio, Kenney, Mustar, Siegel and Wright edited a special issue of Research Policy on entrepreneurial innovation, and this is their introduction to it. Their question is narrow and useful: not whether entrepreneurs innovate, but when and where they do, and what in the surrounding context decides the difference. The answer they build is a framework of contexts, a comparison of two literatures, and a research agenda. - Paper 14: Bækkelund (2021), "Change agency and reproductive agency in the course of industrial path evolution", Regional Studies 55(4). For class 5. In brief: Research on regional industry paths has mostly looked at agency in path creation, so radical change agency has moved to the centre. Bækkelund argues that reproductive agency remains under-conceptualized. It is often read as a source of lock-in or obstruction, while its other roles have scarcely been studied (page 757). The paper therefore introduces a distinction between change agency and reproductive agency, and follows both across the whole life of a path. Its research question is: how do change and reproductive agency vary in the course of path evolution? Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 Why the paper adds a second kind of agency; 3 Phases of a path, and how they were traced; 4 Three villages, three tourism paths; 5 Conclusions and the reference list.
- Paper 15: Garud & Karnøe (2003), "Bricolage versus breakthrough: distributed and embedded agency in technology entrepreneurship", Research Policy 32. For class 5. In brief: Most work on entrepreneurship puts agency in a few exceptional individuals, either because of the traits they possess or because they are heroic figures who overcome the odds. Garud and Karnøe argue instead that technology entrepreneurship is a process distributed across many actors. Their question is how one group of actors deploying modest resources could prevail over another group deploying far superior resources. Wind power is the case. American actors had money and scientific backing, while Danish actors had little of either, yet Danish firms became the world leaders in the market. Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 Why agency is distributed, and why it is embedded; 3 Two wind turbine paths begin; 4 Design, users and the test station: where the paths diverge; 5 The test centre, the regulators, and the two approaches compared; 6 What the comparison shows, and the reference list.

In brief: Regions do not start new industries on empty ground. The authors begin from work showing that new industrial paths grow out of what a region already has: its firms, its universities, its support agencies and its rules. That work describes a regional environment as either enabling or constraining for a new industry. Miörner and Trippl argue that this picture is too static. Their question is how a constraining environment can be turned into an enabling one, and what role key actors play in that change (page 482). Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 Why a constraining region can be turned into an enabling one; 3 Key actors, three forms of power and three modes of change; 4 Scania's games industry, and what held it back; 5 Three new elements: the conference, the school and the cluster; 6 Conclusions and the reference list.
- Paper 9: Hekkert et al. (2007), "Functions of innovation systems: a new approach for analysing technological change", Technological Forecasting and Social Change 74(4). For class 6. In brief: The authors start from a claim about why some technologies spread and others do not. Innovation systems, they argue, are an important determinant of technological change, and the emergence of a new system co-evolves with the technology itself. Their problem is that the usual way of analysing such systems does not capture this. Most studies compare structures, so they describe a system as it stands rather than how it changes, and they explain performance through institutions rather than through what entrepreneurs do (page 414). Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 Why a structural view of innovation systems was not enough; 3 From mapping structure to mapping functions; 4 Seven functions for analysing an innovation system; 5 Motors of change, process analysis and the functionalist worry; 6 Conclusions and the reference list.
- Paper 10: Steen et al. (2024), "CCS technological innovation system dynamics in Norway", International Journal of Greenhouse Gas Control 136. For class 6. In brief: Steen and colleagues ask what are the strengths and weaknesses of the carbon capture and storage innovation system in Norway. The question matters because CCS is vital to most net-zero scenarios and especially important in hard-to-abate sectors, such as energy-intensive processing industries and waste (page 1). Capture is technically possible, but key challenges persist: financing, low carbon prices, transport and storage infrastructure, insufficient regulation and policy, public acceptance, and legitimacy. Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 The question, the framework and the Norwegian setting; 3 Knowledge, experimentation, markets and the direction of search; 4 Resources, legitimacy and the verdict on the functions; 5 Conclusions, limitations and the appendices; 6 The interview guide and the reference list.
- Paper 17: Njøs et al. (2020), "Expanding analyses of path creation: interconnections between territory and technology", Economic Geography 96(3). For class 9. In brief: Njøs, Sjøtun, Jakobsen and Fløysand ask how evolutionary economic geography explains the emergence of new industries, and how the technological innovation system literature can inform its analyses of path creation (page 267). Their starting point is that evolutionary economic geography explains regional development through territorial capabilities, such as knowledge sharing, support structures and industrial policy. They argue it says little about technology itself. Territorial dynamics or technological characteristics alone do not explain path creation. What matters is how the two connect, and how that connection plays out in time and space (page 266). Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 The argument, the EEG gap and the definition of path creation; 3 Regional capabilities, agency and policy, and what TIS adds; 4 The method, the region and the two technologies; 5 The CCS case and the battery case; 6 The comparison, the discussion and the reference list.
- Paper 11: Bargoni et al. (2024), "Crowdfunding and innovation: a bibliometric review and future research agenda", European Journal of Innovation Management 27(4). For bonus class. In brief: The paper asks three questions about a literature that has grown quickly. How has research on crowdfunding and innovation evolved? Which fields and research streams have emerged from the two? And which questions are still open (page 1111)? It matters because earlier mappings of crowdfunding took single aspects, such as one campaign model or the success factors of a campaign. None framed the knowledge structure of the whole field. Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 The question, the three research questions and the search; 3 The three tools, the publication trends and the first two clusters; 4 Social capital, platform innovation, the keyword clusters and the thematic map; 5 The discussion and what the clusters mean for managers; 6 Twenty-five questions for future research; 7 The limitations and the reference list.
- Paper 12: Demattos Guimarães, Maehle & Bonet (2024), "The relational forms of cultural-creative crowdfunding: a typology of practices through mapping platforms in Europe and Latin America", Poetics 105. For bonus class. In brief: The paper asks how cultural-creative crowdfunding has evolved and which models and channels platforms have developed under that umbrella. It matters because the literature is embryonic: the sectoral side of crowdfunding is under-explored, and that there is a gap in understanding and defining cultural-creative platforms (page 2). Their aim is a broader typology of CCCF practices. Their main assumption is that CCCF is a set of diverse practices under the wider crowdfunding umbrella (page 3). Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 The gap, the CCCF concept, the platforms and the policy paradigms; 3 The puzzle, the two Delphi panels and the platform database; 4 What the mapping found: the table, the panels and Figure 3; 5 The discussion, the conclusions and the reference list.
- Paper 13: Quero & Ventura (2019), "Value proposition as a framework for value cocreation in crowdfunding ecosystems", Marketing Theory 19(1). For bonus class. In brief: Quero and Ventura ask whether the ecosystem approach is needed to understand crowdfunding. Their own, broader definition (page 48) makes crowdfunding a business model in which actors cocreate value to carry one actor's proposal forward, by raising money and by other means. They place the argument in service-dominant logic. Their stated aim is to add to two of its foundational premises, FP 7 and FP 10, by showing that the ecosystem approach is required. Three propositions guide the study (page 48). Crowdfunding can be considered a service ecosystem; the Eight Co's model shows the eight ways value propositions are cocreated; and the value proposition approach is a useful tool for analysing cocreation at each level of the ecosystem. Chapters: 1 The paper in brief; 2 The aim, the three propositions and the actors of crowdfunding; 3 The planning framework, the Eight Co's and the two Spanish platforms; 4 The results, the three levels and the discussion; 5 The conclusion and the reference list.

Page 1: This page introduces a conference paper presented at the 5th Nordic Geographers Meeting held in Iceland in June 2013. The authors, Arnt Fløysand, Stig-Erik Jakobsen and Knut Hidle, represent the University of Bergen and Bergen University College. The paper examines the trinity of innovation, exploring relationships between actors, ideas and objects in innovation practice. The authors explicitly note that this draft is a paper in preparation and should not be quoted.

Page 2: Page 2 presents the research problem by exploring innovation as a discursive, material and practical phenomenon. The authors highlight a paradox in innovation studies between context-specific uniqueness and generic abstractions. To bridge ideographic and nomothetic approaches, they propose an analytical model that combines generic innovation properties with contextual specificities. Their epistemological framework builds on actor-network theory from Latour, which highlights interplays between ideas, materiality and practice, combined with realism from Sayer, which distinguishes between necessary and contingent relations.

Page 3: Page 3 establishes the theoretical foundation by integrating critical realism and actor-network theory. From Sayer, the authors adopt the distinction between abstract necessary relations and contingent, place-specific structures. From actor-network theory, they introduce the concept of actants to examine the social, material and immaterial dimensions of innovation. Reviewing the history of innovation, the authors note that the term is positively charged in Western culture, often linked to progress and breaking established paradigms, as discussed by Kuhn, Foucault and Shils.

Page 4: Page 4 traces the history and roots of the word. Godin shows that Plato and Aristotle valued imitation, which was seen as positive until the mid-eighteenth century. Godin also points to Nelson and Winter, who link a firm's innovation to its routines, and to Levitt, for whom imitation is often a survival strategy. The Latin root means to renew, or to change into something new. The authors conclude that the word's original meaning already refers to materiality, idea and practice. Early research, by contrast, counted patents and new products.

Page 5: Page 5 evaluates Hägerstrand's spatial diffusion model, noting that it demonstrated distance delays in information flows among farmers adopting cattle vaccination. However, early diffusion research treated innovation as an objective, measurable entity while neglecting ideas. Later scholars recognized innovation as a sociocultural process. Citing Polanyi, the authors note that the economy operates as an institutional process. Nelson and Winter incorporated organizational learning into evolutionary theory. Finally, systems of innovation perspectives emerged from Freeman, Nelson and Lundvall, emphasizing interactive learning among diverse actors.
