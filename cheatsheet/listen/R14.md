## 2. Regional innovation system
<!-- readings: Fløysand et al. 2022, pp. 15, 16; Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 328; Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 431; Class 3, slides 20–23 -->

The job of this part is the first definition. Slide 40 of Class 9 says to start from the readings and to clarify the concepts. Revision three explains the regional system in full. Here you need the line this question asks for.

In plain words, a regional innovation system is how a region learns, not a list of offices. More precisely, Fløysand and colleagues, 2022, citing Cooke, treat it as interacting subsystems of knowledge infrastructure and commercial exploitation. The same reading, citing Stuck and colleagues, sets those subsystems in knowledge, industry and governance. The model answer lists firms, universities, research bodies and public authorities as if that list were the definition. Prefer Cooke, and name Stuck for the three-part picture.

History sits inside the system. Fagerberg and colleagues, 2009, citing Arthur, call that path dependency. Earlier development favours some solutions and holds others back.

The Norwegian case is the pair of regions. Slides 20 to 23 of Class 3 describe Hordaland as diversified, with maritime technology and seafood, and with a broad knowledge base in Bergen. Rogaland is petroleum-specialised. Fløysand and colleagues say a regional system can enable innovation and can also constrain it. The two regions share a green vision and still diverge.

One way to say it: By a regional innovation system, I understand interacting knowledge and exploitation subsystems, set in a region.

Remember: Cooke for the subsystems, Stuck for the three parts, and the two regions for the contrast.

## 3. Institutional work
<!-- readings: Fløysand et al. 2022, pp. 15, 22; Class 9, slide 31 -->

The job of this part is the second definition. Slide 40 of Class 9 still applies. Define the term as the reading does, and keep the name it is borrowed from.

In plain words, institutional work is what actors do to the rules, not the rules themselves. More precisely, Fløysand and colleagues, 2022, citing Lawrence and colleagues, call it the physical and mental work actors do to affect institutions. That work can maintain, create, change or disrupt them. The model answer calls it intentional action and leaves Lawrence out. Credit Lawrence, and stay with the four kinds of effort.

The same reading shows both directions. The work can support a new industry, and it can keep an old practice in place. In diversified Hordaland, collective work across clusters legitimised a green shift. In petroleum-specialised Rogaland, individual work, tied to individual strategic leadership, delegitimised it. Both regions depended on petroleum, and both voiced greener ambitions.

Slide 31 of Class 9 states the awkward result. The work aims at a green shift, and yet oil and gas still dominates, unintentionally. Actors can reproduce the old rules while saying they want change.

One way to say it: By institutional work, I understand the effort actors make to maintain, create, change or disrupt rules.

Remember: Lawrence for the term, and the two regions for the two directions of the work.

## 4. Sustainability
<!-- readings: Schlaile et al. 2017, pp. 1, 4, 5; Class 9, slides 27, 29; Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 17 -->

The job of this part is the third definition. Slides 34 and 35 of Class 9 ask for the definition the course uses, not a slogan from outside it.

In plain words, sustainability says how things ought to be, so it is not a neutral label for growth. Slide 29 of Class 9 gives three definitions from the UN, from 2023. Economic sustainability is the efficient use and fair distribution of resources. Social sustainability is the well-being, equity and inclusion of communities. Environmental sustainability means using nature without harming it beyond its capacity to regenerate. The model answer uses a present-needs sentence instead. Leave that sentence out. The slide does not give it.

Schlaile and colleagues, 2017, add why the word is normative. Their opening calls for research on directionality, legitimacy and responsibility. On the exam-day sheet, sustainability also includes ecological continuity, justice within and between generations, and quality of life. Slide 27 says technical solutions alone cannot reach sustainability goals. They take the term dedicated innovation system from Pyka. It means a system pointed at those goals on purpose.

A weaker answer would equate sustainability with any new green technique. A stronger answer would use the three 2023 lines and say the aim has to be chosen.

One way to say it: By sustainability, I understand the three 2023 lines for economy, society and environment, treated as a chosen aim.

Remember: the three UN lines, not the present-needs sentence, and Pyka for a system that aims at them.

## 5. How the three concepts connect
<!-- readings: Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15; Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 328; Schlaile et al. 2017, p. 5; Class 9, slide 41 -->

The job of this part is to join the three terms into one argument. Slide 41 of Class 9 says to sum up and to answer the question you were asked.

There are three links, and each one stays inside a reading. First, the regional system is the learning in a place, with knowledge and exploitation interacting, and with a history that favours some paths. Fløysand and Jakobsen, 2016, name four trajectories. Exhaustion, extension, renewal and creation. Extension is more of the same. Creation is a new industry. Second, institutional work is how actors maintain those paths or move them. In the two regions the same green aim met two kinds of work, and the outcomes diverged. Third, sustainability is the direction of that move. A new technique is not enough. Schlaile and colleagues say a conventional path need not serve a social vision.

A memory aid of our own: the region, the work, then the aim.

A weaker answer would define the three terms in three sealed boxes. A stronger answer would show the work inside one regional system, aimed at one kind of change.

One way to say it: The region is the system, the work moves or holds the path, and sustainability is the aim.

Remember: three terms, one argument, and the two regions are where they meet.

## 6. Traps and upgrades
<!-- readings: Fløysand et al. 2022, pp. 15, 16; Class 9, slides 29, 43; Schlaile et al. 2017, p. 4 -->

The job of this part is to retire the lines that sound finished and are not what the course says. Slide 43 rewards readings that are understood.

The first trap is the model answer's regional system as a list of firms, universities, research bodies and public authorities. Instead, use the Cooke line that Fløysand and colleagues cite. Interacting subsystems of knowledge and exploitation. Name Stuck and colleagues for knowledge, industry and governance.

The second trap is institutional work with no origin, or as a vague intention. Instead, cite Lawrence and colleagues. The work can maintain, create, change or disrupt institutions.

The third trap is the present-needs sentence as the definition of sustainability. Instead, use the three 2023 UN definitions on slide 29 of Class 9.

The fourth trap is to treat specialisation as a measured drop in investment. Instead, say what the reading says. In Rogaland, specialised individual work delegitimised a green shift. The reading does not measure investment.

The fifth trap is a battery life-cycle sum, or a social-effects test of capture, offered as the way to judge sustainability. Instead, leave both out. No course reading makes that assessment.

Remember: prefer the corrected course line, and say so when a model answer goes past it.
