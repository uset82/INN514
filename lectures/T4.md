This fourth lecture explores a different perspective on innovation. Earlier lectures examined territorial systems across nations and regions. Here, Nora Geirsdotter Bækkelund puts the entrepreneur at the centre. Bækkelund examines how the environment around founders shapes their ability to build new ventures.

The lecture begins by connecting innovation with entrepreneurship. Innovation means putting new combinations and novel ideas into practice. The Austrian economist Joseph Schumpeter, in early work from 1912, argued that the entrepreneur is the central figure who innovates. Robert Baron, in a 2012 book, defined entrepreneurship as what happens when people use creativity, resourcefulness, energy and talent to develop something new and useful. That activity creates economic and social value.

Yet the lecture makes an important distinction: entrepreneurship is not always innovative. On page 1097 of their article, Autio and colleagues point out that the general public and policymakers often confuse the two terms. They support one in the hope of getting the other. In reality, most new businesses simply copy existing models. A standard shop is an enterprise, but it rarely introduces a novel combination.

To show why innovation matters over time, slide 3 presents long waves of economic prosperity. The model comes from Allianz in 2010 and builds on Kondratieff waves. Over two centuries, five technological waves transformed industry, from steam engines and railways to electricity, automobiles and computers. We are now in the sixth wave, which began around 2010. This wave is driven by renewable energies and smart grids.

In modern business, fast-growing startups valued at more than one billion dollars are called unicorns. Slide 4 displays examples of these firms, such as Tesla, OpenAI, Discord, and the warehouse robotics company Autostore. But unicorns do not appear evenly across the globe. Slide 5 presents data compiled by Acs and colleagues, alongside a map from Stam and Saberi from 2017. They show that high-growth startups cluster tightly in a handful of global cities. San Francisco leads the world table with 34 unicorns, followed by Beijing and New York. This raises a pressing question. Why do certain places produce so many high-growth firms while other places produce very few?

## 2. Why context matters: from individuals to ecosystems
<!-- slides: 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 -->
<!-- readings: Autio et al. 2014, pp. 1098, 1099, 1100; Spigel & Harrison 2018; Stam & van de Ven 2021, p. 809 -->

Why do some places produce many successful startups while others struggle? The lecture answers with a single word: context.

On slide 6, the lecture highlights a core insight from Autio and colleagues, published in 2014. On page 1099 of their article, they write that it is the context that regulates what individuals and teams get to see, what choices they are likely to make, and what the outcomes of those choices are likely to be. Human beings never act in an isolated vacuum. The rules, relationships and resources around a founder shape every step of the entrepreneurial journey.

Slide 7 asks what factors around the entrepreneur influence innovative entrepreneurship. To answer, slide 8 presents the organizing framework from Autio and colleagues, published on page 1098. On page 1100, the authors identify four overlapping contexts. First is the organizational context, including company cultures and learning habits. Second is the institutional and policy context, covering formal regulations and informal social norms. Third is the industry and technological context, including digital platforms and technical standards. Fourth is the social context, including personal networks. These four contexts operate within a temporal context of historical time, across local, regional, national and global levels. Together, they shape both the type of entrepreneurial innovation and its performance.

Slide 9 shows how academic research moved towards this contextual view. Historically, entrepreneurship research focused almost entirely on the individual. Scholars examined personal traits, psychology, cognition, mindset and founder identity. But looking at individuals in isolation could not explain why similar people succeed in one region and fail in another. Meanwhile, innovation scholars studied systems, developing theories of industrial clusters and regional innovation systems. Yet as Spigel and Harrison point out in their 2018 article, cluster and system models were designed primarily for large, established firms. They pay less attention to the birth of brand-new ventures.

This gap gave rise to the concept of entrepreneurial ecosystems. This perspective places entrepreneurs directly at the centre. It focuses on the local entrepreneurial community rather than only on formal state support. On page 809 of their 2021 article, Stam and van de Ven note that the concept is growing in popularity, but that it remains loosely defined and measured.

Slide 10 provides a classic definition. Quoting Stam and Spigel from 2016, an entrepreneurial ecosystem is a set of interdependent actors and factors coordinated in such a way that they enable productive entrepreneurship. The slide leaves us with two open questions: who are these actors, and what factors coordinate them?

## 3. Two models of the ecosystem
<!-- slides: 11, 12, 13 -->
<!-- readings: Stam & van de Ven 2021, pp. 810, 813, 814; Spigel & Harrison 2018, p. 152 -->

To turn the broad concept of an ecosystem into a practical framework, researchers have developed analytical models. The lecture introduces two influential examples.

The first model comes from Stam and van de Ven in 2021, shown on slide 11. On page 810, they build on van de Ven's work from 1993 on the infrastructure for entrepreneurship. On page 813, they present a framework divided into two main layers.

At the base sit institutional arrangements. These are formal institutions, such as legal rules and property rights; informal culture, such as social attitudes towards ambition; and networks linking local actors. Resting on these institutions are resource endowments. These include physical infrastructure, market demand, intermediaries who connect people, talent, scientific knowledge, leadership, and access to finance.

The model links these parts through three propositions, set out on page 814. Proposition one states that the elements are mutually interdependent and co-evolve. Proposition two, which the authors call upward causation, states that the elements together explain the level of productive entrepreneurship in a territory, the ecosystem's output. Productive entrepreneurship means activity that creates net economic and social value. Proposition three, downward causation, describes a feedback loop: successful entrepreneurial outputs feed back into the ecosystem, enriching local institutions and resources over time.

The second model, presented on slide 12, comes from Spigel and Harrison. The slide also cites a 2016 paper by Stam and Spigel. The model sorts the ecosystem into three interdependent elements: cultural, social and material.

Cultural elements provide the shared outlook. They include positive social attitudes towards business creation, inspiring local success stories, and an acceptance of failure as a natural step in learning.

Social elements provide the human connections and resources. They include risk capital, skilled workers, and experienced mentors who advise young founders.

Material elements provide the physical and organizational backbone. They include startup incubators and accelerators, university research centres, transport infrastructure, and targeted public policies.

Slide 13 adds a short video titled How to Build a Thriving Entrepreneurial Ecosystem Anywhere. On page 152 of their article, Spigel and Harrison explain that the global popularity of ecosystems was accelerated by practitioners. In particular, venture investor Brad Feld wrote about startup communities in Boulder, Colorado, while Daniel Isenberg wrote about ecosystem domains. Both argued that a flourishing startup scene depends on community cooperation rather than bureaucratic planning.

## 4. Ecosystems develop over time: resources and networks
<!-- slides: 14, 15, 16 -->
<!-- readings: Spigel & Harrison 2018, pp. 158, 159, 160, 162, 163 -->
<!-- sourced: Flint | slide 16, quadrant IV in the diagram -->

An ecosystem is not a static machine that you assemble from a checklist. It is an evolving process. On slide 14, the lecture turns to the process perspective developed by Spigel and Harrison.

To understand how ecosystems grow, the authors identify two central dimensions on page 158: resources and networks. Simply having resources inside a region is not enough. The lecture points out the contrast between Cambridge, Massachusetts, and San Francisco, California. Both regions possess world-class universities and capital. But San Francisco grew into an entrepreneurial leader because founders had access to dense social networks where resources circulate freely.

On page 159, the authors explain that a founder's capabilities and credibility depend on social relations. Critical resources are wrapped up in informal social networks. If a founder is not trusted by the local community, capital and talent remain out of reach. Learning between entrepreneurs, mutual trust, and supportive cultural attitudes keep resources moving.

A central engine of ecosystem growth is resource recycling, discussed on page 159. When successful entrepreneurs build a company and reach a profitable exit, their journey does not end. In a well-functioning ecosystem, founders reinvest their wealth, knowledge, and prestige locally. On page 160, the authors note that successful founders remain in the region as angel investors, serial entrepreneurs, advisors, and mentors. They help the next wave of startups. Even when a firm fails, its workers and technical insights are recycled into other local businesses, provided the local culture treats failure as an educational milestone.

Slide 15 illustrates this evolution with Figure 1 from Spigel and Harrison, published on page 162. An ecosystem moves through distinct stages. It starts as a nascent ecosystem, where connectivity is low, resources leak away to other regions, and little recycling occurs. As connectivity improves and dealmakers emerge to link different groups, it becomes a strengthening ecosystem, attracting external resources. Over time, it can mature into a resilient ecosystem that survives industry downturns through dense networks and continuous resource creation. But if an economic shock breaks local connections and talent departs, it can decline into a weakened ecosystem.

Slide 16 shows Figure 2 from page 163, classifying ecosystems across network strength and resource munificence. Strong ecosystems, like Silicon Valley and Boulder, combine abundant resources with well-functioning networks. Arid ecosystems, like Accra and Lagos, have strong networks but sparse resources. Irrigated ecosystems, like Calgary and Aberdeen, have abundant financial resources from resource industries but poor network connectivity for new startups. Finally, weak ecosystems, like Hull and Flint, struggle with both sparse resources and fragmented networks.

## 5. Can public policy build an ecosystem?
<!-- slides: 17, 18 -->
<!-- readings: Spigel & Harrison 2018, p. 164; Autio et al. 2014, p. 1099 -->

Can public policy build an entrepreneurial ecosystem? This is one of the most debated questions in modern economic development, and slide 17 tackles it directly.

The lecture's primary conclusion is straightforward: ecosystems must grow from the bottom up. The most decisive elements of a healthy ecosystem come from entrepreneurs themselves. Government officials cannot create mutual trust, informal social networks, or a culture of ambition by administrative decree. On page 164, Spigel and Harrison stress that policy makers should support existing entrepreneurial communities rather than attempt to engineer ecosystems from scratch.

This does not mean the public sector is powerless. A table on page 1099 of the article by Autio and colleagues compares three views, each with its own policy emphasis. The national innovation system view supports research with subsidy programmes. The entrepreneurship view supports the creation and growth of firms. The view the authors argue for, entrepreneurial innovation in context, aims to help whole entrepreneurial ecosystems develop. Slide 17 lists practical ways the state can support the entrepreneurial community.

First, it can create arenas and meeting places where entrepreneurs meet and build social ties. Second, it can finance incubators, accelerators and supporting infrastructure. Third, it can make risk capital and venture funds available. Fourth, it can offer training programmes.

Fifth, it can create incentives for contact between research and entrepreneurs, and for academic spin-offs. Sixth, it can influence culture by speaking well of entrepreneurship. Seventh, it can use immigration policy, such as start-up visas in the United States or work visas in Norway. The slide adds that Spigel and Harrison have a good chapter on policy.

On slide 18, the lecturer returns to the core question: what factors around the entrepreneur influence innovative entrepreneurship? This reminds us that policy works best when it removes bottlenecks in the surrounding environment, allowing private initiative and community networks to thrive.

## 6. A founder house in Bergen: an ecosystem in practice
<!-- slides: 19, 20 -->
<!-- readings: none new -->

<!-- sourced: Jahnebakken | slide 19, image caption at bottom of slide -->

To see how these concepts apply to our own region, slide 19 introduces a local case: a new investor and founder house in Bergen.

The slide features an article from the Norwegian business news website Shifter with the headline: Investor couple buys villas to make Bergen a bit more like Silicon Valley. The subtitle explains that the investor couple Kristin and Johan Odfjell will turn two old villas into an investor house and a founder house. The caption shows how the villas in Jahnebakken in Bergen will look once they are renovated.

The lecturer uses this real-world initiative to pose two questions for class reflection. First, what does this article reveal about Bergen's innovation ecosystem? What key actors, cultural attitudes, material resources, and social networks are visible? Second, do you believe this investor and founder house can make a genuine difference to local entrepreneurial activity, and if so, how?

Here is one way of our own to approach the questions, not from the lecture. Use the three kinds of element from chapter 3. On the material side, the house gives founders a physical place to work and meet. On the social side, it puts investors and founders under one roof. That could help resources move through networks, which Spigel and Harrison see as the key to a growing ecosystem. On the cultural side, ask whether it creates visible success stories and role models. Whether it makes a real difference is the open question the slide leaves you with.

Slide 20 connects this case to a broader framework: Daniel Isenberg's classic 2009 diagram of the domains of an entrepreneurship ecosystem. Isenberg places entrepreneurship at the centre of six interconnected domains: policy, finance, culture, supports, human capital, and markets. Each domain contains distinct components. Finance includes angel investors, private equity, and venture capital. Culture includes tolerance of failure and visible success stories. Supports include physical infrastructure and technical experts. Human capital includes skilled workers and universities. The Bergen villa project touches almost every domain, showing how private actors attempt to build regional entrepreneurial momentum.

## 7. Critique and the three readings
<!-- slides: 21, 22, 23, 24 -->
<!-- readings: Spigel & Harrison 2018, pp. 151, 158; Stam & van de Ven 2021, p. 809; Autio et al. 2014, p. 1097 -->

The lecture concludes with a critical assessment of the entrepreneurial ecosystem framework and a review of the three assigned readings.

On slide 21, the lecturer notes that while the ecosystem perspective offers valuable insights into the context of business creation, the concept faces serious academic criticism. On page 158 of their 2018 article, Spigel and Harrison outline three major weaknesses.

First, the ecosystem concept is tautological. An ecosystem is defined as a place with successful entrepreneurship, and whenever successful entrepreneurship is found, researchers assume a strong ecosystem must be present. The cause and the outcome are circular.

Second, the framework is often purely descriptive. It presents what critics call a laundry list of factors without explaining how cause and effect work, or how the various components cohere into a functioning system.

Third, the concept lacks a clear geography. There is ongoing confusion about the correct level of analysis. Scholars disagree on whether an ecosystem should be studied at the level of a city, a region, an entire country, or even non-spatial units like corporate networks and global value chains.

Slide 22 addresses this challenge by contrasting abstraction with contextualization. Grouping factors into cultural, social, and material categories is an abstract exercise. To produce meaningful research, scholars must investigate what actually happens inside real interpersonal relationships in specific local settings.

Slide 23 summarizes what each assigned reading contributes.

Spigel and Harrison, in their 2018 article, explain what the theory is about and trace its history. They clarify how ecosystems differ from clusters and regional innovation systems, and present a process-oriented theory based on resources and networks.

Autio and colleagues, in their 2014 article, examine which contextual factors shape entrepreneurial innovation and explore how public policies can nurture ambitious startups.

Stam and van de Ven, in their 2021 article, review earlier contextual literature, provide an operational framework linking institutional arrangements and resource endowments to productive entrepreneurship, and offer tools to measure and benchmark ecosystems across territories.

Slide 24 provides the lecturer's contact address for further discussion.

Here is an exam tip. In December 2024, the exam asked what you understand by innovation systems, sustainable transformation, institutions, and agency. You can use the entrepreneurial ecosystem perspective to explain agency: individual and collective entrepreneurs act as deliberate change agents, but their choices and success remain bound by local institutional rules and social networks.
