## 1. The theme in one minute
<!-- readings: Hekkert et al. 2007, pp. 415, 416; Steen et al. 2024, p. 2 -->

In plain words, a technological innovation system is the group of firms, researchers and rules that gather around one technology and decide whether it grows.

More precisely, Hekkert and colleagues, 2007, take their definition from Carlsson and Stankiewicz. A technological innovation system is a network of agents that create, spread and use a technology under shared institutions. The starting point is the technology itself, not a region and not a sector.

Why does the course care? Because the exam asks how a technology can promote or inhibit sustainable transformation. The functions turn that big question into small ones. You rate each function from strong to weak, and then you can point to the weak one and say what policy should do.

Steen and colleagues, 2024, apply the same idea to carbon capture in Norway. They add one function of their own, materialization, because capture needs pipes, ships and storage sites, and not only knowledge.

Remember: one technology, its actors and its rules, and the functions that show where the system is strong.

## 2. The key ideas
<!-- readings: Hekkert et al. 2007, p. 421; Steen et al. 2024, p. 2 -->

The first idea is that a system should be studied through what happens inside it. Hekkert and colleagues, 2007, call the relevant activities functions. A function is an activity that results in technological change. Their complaint about earlier work is simple. Most studies describe the structure of a system as it stands, and they explain performance through institutions. They do not show how the system moves.

The second idea is the list. Hekkert and colleagues, 2007, name seven functions. Entrepreneurial activity. Knowledge development. Knowledge diffusion through networks. Guidance of the search. Market formation. Resource mobilization. The creation of legitimacy.

The third idea is that the list can be extended. Steen and colleagues, 2024, study carbon capture in Norway and add an eighth function, materialization. Their reason is practical. Carbon capture depends on large physical infrastructure, and the usual seven say little about building it.

The fourth idea is that the functions pull each other. A strong function often lifts its neighbours, and a weak one can drag them down. Hekkert and colleagues, 2007, expect a non-linear model with many interactions, not a tidy checklist.

Remember: functions are activities, seven of them are standard, and they interact.

## 3. The model, step by step
<!-- readings: Hekkert et al. 2007, pp. 421–426 -->

There are seven functions, and it helps to say what each one covers. First, entrepreneurial activity: firms and founders trying new things. Second, knowledge development: research, learning and the knowledge a technology needs. Third, knowledge diffusion through networks: how that knowledge moves between actors.

Fourth, guidance of the search: the expectations and goals that tell actors where to aim. Fifth, market formation: the creation of a market where none exists yet. Sixth, resource mobilization: money, people and infrastructure coming in. Seventh, the creation of legitimacy: acceptance, and the sense that the technology belongs.

Steen and colleagues, 2024, add materialization. That is the building of the physical base, such as pipes, plants and storage.

Hekkert and colleagues, 2007, also describe how a system starts to move. They call the starting patterns motors of change. Motor A begins with lobbying for a market, and then entrepreneurial activity follows. Motor B begins with lobbying for research resources, and then expectations rise. Motor C begins with guidance of the search, and then resources and knowledge follow.

Remember: seven functions, one addition for physical infrastructure, and three motors that can start a system moving.

## 4. Norway examples
<!-- readings: Steen et al. 2024, pp. 4, 10; Njøs et al. 2020, pp. 281–282 -->

Norway gives you ready examples for both the strong and the weak side.

Longship and Northern Lights are the two large projects that carry carbon capture forward. Steen and colleagues, 2024, treat them as the main exceptions to weak materialization, because they are large projects now being built with state support.

The same study shows the weakness. Market formation stays weak, because costs are high and the emissions trading system does not yet justify investment. Knowledge is strong, but diffusion is limited, since each project is customised and firms protect their ideas.

Mongstad is the older example. Steen and colleagues, 2024, describe an earlier plan for a full scale plant at the refinery and the power station.

Maritime batteries show a system that moved faster. Njøs and colleagues, 2020, describe how a cluster set a direction and public procurement created a market, and how some seventy ferry lines followed. The same study shows the contrast. Carbon capture stayed in an early phase, with few dedicated firms and no market.

Remember: Longship and Northern Lights for the strong side, market formation for the weak side.

## 5. How the exam asks about it
<!-- readings: Hekkert et al. 2007, p. 423; Steen et al. 2024, p. 9 -->

Two past questions fit this theme. The June question from 2025 asks how technological innovation systems can be guided to promote sustainable transformation. The December question from 2024 asks how technologies in innovation systems can promote or inhibit it. Notice the words promote and inhibit in the second one. An answer that shows only one side is half an answer.

Here are the moves for the guided question. Open by saying that you will use the functions to find where guidance can act. Then define the system and name the functions, with their source. Then choose a case. Rate each function as strong, weak or intermediate, and point to the weak one. Finally, say what policy could do about it, and what it cannot do.

For the other question, add the second side. A technology can promote transformation when its functions are fulfilled. It can inhibit transformation when a weak function, such as an uncosted market, holds the rest back.

Say once, in your own words, that answers can be tested for plagiarism. Learn the moves, then write them yourself.

Remember: rate the functions, name the weak one, and show both sides.
