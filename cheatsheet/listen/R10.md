## Paper 1: Fagerberg, Mowery and Verspagen, 2009
<!-- readings: Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 431 -->

The paper asks how Norway grew fast while spending little on research. The answer is a broad national innovation system, institutions described as the rules of the game, and a history that favoured resource industries. Norway built petroleum suppliers instead of suffering the Dutch disease. The authors trace three industrial paths and warn that the era of cheap oil and cheap hydropower is ending. The authors also show that Norwegian firms cooperate closely in innovation, a strength that standard measures miss. Their warning is that complacency is the real risk, because the old growth is running out and the cheap power with it.

Remember: a broad system, stable rules and a prepared history.

<!-- check: What explains Norway's paradox in this paper? | A broad innovation system, institutions as rules and path dependency. -->

## Paper 2: Fløysand, Jakobsen and Hidle, 2013
<!-- readings: Fløysand et al. 2013, PDF p. 9 -->

This conference paper treats innovation as a meeting of actors, objects and ideas, which the authors call the trinity of innovation. They borrow the idea from Schwarz and Thompson and add actants from Latour, so that tools and nature count as actors. A company, its tools and its tasks form a practical triangle. The paper is in preparation, so it is paraphrased here and never quoted. The paper is conceptual, so its value for the exam is the vocabulary it gives you for describing practice. Its model is a triangle of company, tools and tasks, and each side is a link that can be studied on its own.

Remember: actors, objects and ideas, and a triangle of company, tools and tasks.

<!-- check: What are the three parts of the trinity? | Actors, objects and ideas, or company, tools and tasks in the practical triangle. -->

## Paper 3: Fløysand and colleagues, 2022
<!-- readings: Fløysand et al. 2022, p. 15 -->

The paper compares Hordaland and Rogaland, two petroleum regions facing a green shift. It combines institutional work with evolutionary economic geography. In Hordaland, collective work across a diversified system legitimized the shift. In Rogaland, individual leadership in a specialized system did not. The finding that matters most is that institutional work aimed at change can also sustain the old industry, and oil and gas still dominates both regions. The authors add that a thick and diversified system is a potential advantage, not a guarantee. The two cases also differ in industrial history, since maritime and salmon farming are stronger in Hordaland.

Remember: two regions, two kinds of work, and an outcome nobody intended.

<!-- check: What did collective work do in Hordaland? | It legitimized a green shift across clusters. -->

## Paper 4: Schlaile and colleagues, 2017
<!-- readings: Schlaile et al. 2017, p. 4 -->

The paper asks what innovation systems would look like if they took sustainability seriously. The authors want a shift in the assumptions that decide what counts as progress, and they call the alternative a dedicated innovation system, a term from Pyka. They add transformative innovation, from Steward, and transformational failures, from Weber and Rohracher. Three questions follow: what to change, who decides and who answers. The strands that try to answer them are still scattered. They also name three failures that policy has to address: weak direction, weak legitimacy and weak responsibility. The authors also want the normativity of sustainability inside the system, not beside it.

Remember: a dedicated system, three questions and a scattered literature.

<!-- check: What are the three questions of a dedicated system? | Directionality, legitimacy and responsibility. -->

## Paper 5: Fløysand and Jakobsen, 2016
<!-- readings: Fløysand & Jakobsen 2016, p. 324 -->

This Norwegian book chapter explains regional renewal in Western Norway. Renewal has three levels, industry, firm and individual, and a path can be exhausted, extended, renewed or created. Related variety is the resource that lets a region combine what it has. The chapter is a scan, so it is paraphrased and never quoted. It shows a region with strong clusters and some new green initiatives. The chapter also shows that ten of eighteen western areas score above the national median for related variety. The chapter studies related variety with an index across economic areas, and most of the west scores above the median.

Remember: three levels of renewal and four trajectories for a path.

<!-- check: What are the three levels of regional renewal? | Industry, firm and individual. -->

## Paper 6: Spigel and Harrison, 2018
<!-- readings: Spigel & Harrison 2018, p. 158 -->

The paper argues that an entrepreneurial ecosystem is a process, not a thing. Entrepreneurs acquire resources, knowledge and support through it, and access matters more than presence. The authors separate munificence, how many resources exist, from functionality, how easily they can be reached. They also describe recycling, where founders return as investors and mentors, and they sketch four ecosystem types. The authors also describe four ecosystem types, from strong to weak, on two axes. The paper is conceptual, and its propositions are offered to guide research rather than to report results, which is why they read as a research agenda and why their claims stay modest.

Remember: a process, access over presence, and recycling.

<!-- check: What is the difference between munificence and functionality? | How many resources exist, and how easily they can be reached. -->

## Paper 7: Stam and van de Ven, 2021
<!-- readings: Stam & van de Ven 2021, p. 813 -->

The paper defines the ecosystem, gives it ten elements and builds an index for the twelve Dutch provinces. The elements sit in three groups: institutional arrangements, resources and productive entrepreneurship. The authors refuse to regress output on ten separate causes, because the system is one system and the elements are entangled. Their index explains a good part of the variation in high growth firms, and they call it an indication rather than proof. The authors name their own limits: twelve regions, three points in time and one country. The index puts an average province at ten, and Utrecht reaches more than twelve while Drenthe stays below six.

Remember: ten elements in three groups and one index.

<!-- check: What are the three groups of elements? | Institutional arrangements, resources and output. -->

## Paper 8: Autio and colleagues, 2014
<!-- readings: Autio et al. 2014, p. 1100 -->

This is the editors' introduction to a special issue on entrepreneurial innovation. The authors ask when and where new ventures innovate, and they answer with context. Selection effects decide who becomes an entrepreneur, and strategic choice effects decide what those who enter attempt. Six contexts surround the entrepreneur, from industry and technology to social setting, in time and space. Innovation, they insist, is not the same as entrepreneurship. Fewer than thirty percent of new ventures report products that are new to their customers. The authors also say the framework opens a research agenda rather than a test, and they call for work across the six contexts.

Remember: selection, strategic choice and six contexts.

<!-- check: What do selection effects decide? | Who becomes an entrepreneur. -->

## Paper 14: Bækkelund, 2021
<!-- readings: Bækkelund 2021, p. 758 -->

The paper follows the whole life of an industry path in three Norwegian villages, Flåm, Odda and Sogndal. It separates change agency, which alters a path, from reproductive agency, which keeps it going, and gives three forms of each. Reproductive work is stronger before a path forms and in path development, and change work is stronger in path creation. A critical juncture helps, and it needs an opportunity space. Flåm shows the most change. The author also notes that the cases come only from rural tourism, an industry with its own features. The three villages share formal institutions and surroundings, which makes the comparison close to a most similar design, and it makes the differences in outcome stand out.

Remember: two kinds of agency across the life of a path.

<!-- check: When is change agency stronger? | In path creation. -->

## Paper 15: Garud and Karnøe, 2003
<!-- readings: Garud & Karnøe 2003, p. 278 -->

The paper asks how Danish wind power actors with modest resources beat better funded American actors. Its answer is distributed and embedded agency. The inputs come from actors in different domains, and the path then enables and constrains the actors who built it. The authors contrast bricolage, resourcefulness in steady steps, with breakthrough, an attempt at dramatic outcomes. Figure 2 puts the artifact at the centre of four embedding processes. The authors show that the same actors can be enabled and constrained by the path they built, which is why bricolage rewards patience. Their evidence is a historical comparison of two national paths, so the argument rests on the detail of the two cases.

Remember: distributed agency, bricolage and a path that constrains its builders.

<!-- check: What does bricolage mean? | Resourcefulness and improvisation with what is at hand, in steady steps. -->

## Paper 16: Miörner and Trippl, 2017
<!-- readings: Miörner & Trippl 2017, p. 486 -->

The paper asks how a constraining region can be turned into an enabling one. The answer is three modes of change: layering, adaptation and novel application. A few key individuals combined them across scales in Scania, where the games industry grew around the Nordic Game Conference, the Game Assembly and Game City. Power decides the mode. Actors with power can layer, and weaker actors adapt or reapply what exists. The findings rest on one case. The authors also say there is no hierarchy between the modes, since a novel application can do as much as layering. The authors base their argument on document studies and thirteen interviews from twenty fifteen.

Remember: three modes of change and a few actors with power.

<!-- check: Which mode adds a new element to the support structure? | Layering. -->

## Paper 9: Hekkert and colleagues, 2007
<!-- readings: Hekkert et al. 2007, p. 421 -->

The paper turns the study of innovation systems from structures to activities. It names seven functions, from entrepreneurial activity to the creation of legitimacy, and it argues that they interact. A few functions can start a virtuous cycle, which the authors call a motor of change. They keep the word function for its heuristic value and reject the assumptions of functionalism. The method they propose is process analysis, or history event analysis. The authors also expect the functions to interact, so the model is not a checklist but a set of loops. The paper also proposes process analysis, which maps events onto the functions over time.

Remember: seven functions, three motors and a heuristic word.

<!-- check: Name three of the seven functions. | Entrepreneurial activity, knowledge development and market formation, among others. -->

## Paper 10: Steen and colleagues, 2024
<!-- readings: Steen et al. 2024, p. 9 -->

The paper assesses the carbon capture system in Norway with the functions. It adds an eighth function, materialization, because capture needs large physical infrastructure. Knowledge is strong and diffusion is limited, because projects are customised and firms protect their ideas. Market formation and materialization stay weak, with Longship and Northern Lights as the exceptions. Legitimacy rises as capture moves from fossil energy into waste and process industry. Momentum, the authors say, appears positive. The authors also name three limits, including that other interviewees might have produced different results. The authors also call for a policy mix that goes beyond one flagship project, because a single project cannot carry a whole system, however large it is.

Remember: eight functions, weak materialization and a positive momentum.

<!-- check: Which function did the authors add? | Materialization. -->

## Paper 17: Njøs and colleagues, 2020
<!-- readings: Njøs et al. 2020, p. 268 -->

The paper argues that territory and technology have to be analysed together. It compares carbon capture and maritime batteries in the same region, and finds them in different phases. Batteries reached green path creation through regional competence, a cluster and public procurement, and some seventy ferry lines followed. Carbon capture stayed in preformation, with few dedicated firms and no market. The regional level, the authors suggest, can matter less than expected. The authors also leave three questions open, including whether green path creation differs from other kinds. The paper also argues that narratives in the battery case have been studied by neither literature.

Remember: two technologies, one region and two phases.

<!-- check: What moved maritime batteries forward? | Regional competence, a cluster and public procurement. -->

## Paper 11: Bargoni and colleagues, 2024
<!-- readings: Bargoni et al. 2024, p. 1112 -->

This bibliometric review maps the field where crowdfunding meets innovation. It reduces more than two thousand records to one hundred and fifty studies and finds three citation clusters: innovativeness, openness and platform innovation. Crowdfunding is treated as more than money, since it lets users act like venture capitalists. The authors separate incremental from radical innovativeness and close with a long list of open questions. The authors also name two limits: Scopus alone as a source, and English language articles only. The authors also note that two thousand five hundred and forty four records were screened down to that set, which is worth saying when an examiner asks how the field was mapped.

Remember: a map of the field, three clusters and an agenda for the next studies.

<!-- check: How many studies did the review keep? | One hundred and fifty. -->

## Paper 12: Demattos Guimarães, Maehle and Bonet, 2024
<!-- readings: Demattos Guimarães et al. 2024, p. 8 -->

The paper builds a typology of cultural and creative crowdfunding. It codes eighty nine platforms, seventy nine in Europe and ten in Latin America, on six dimensions such as country, business model and institutional structure. Two expert panels reached consensus and disagreed with each other. The main output is a map from institutional structure to practices. The authors leave out platforms from the US and invite a North American study. Each panel reached consensus, and the two panels differed, which the authors read as support for their puzzle. Most of the platforms are young, and about half use a hybrid business model rather than a single one.

Remember: eighty nine platforms, six dimensions and one typology of practices.

<!-- check: How many platforms did the authors code? | Eighty nine. -->

## Paper 13: Quero and Ventura, 2019
<!-- readings: Quero & Ventura 2019, p. 51 -->

The paper asks whether crowdfunding needs the ecosystem approach to be understood. It answers yes, and places the argument in service dominant logic. Value propositions are cocreated by several actors, and the authors adapt a five step framework and a list of eight ways to cocreate. The ecosystem has three levels, micro, meso and macro. The two platform directors they interviewed added public institutions to the list of actors. The authors also find that associations and public institutions bring institutionalisation at the macro level. The authors also stress that the value proposition is a tool for analysing cocreation at each level.

Remember: a service ecosystem, five steps and three levels.

<!-- check: Which actor did the directors add? | Public institutions. -->
