## 1. The theme in one minute
<!-- readings: Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 431; Fløysand et al. 2013, PDF p. 9 -->

In plain words, an innovation system is the whole way a country learns. It is not only the laboratories and the universities. It is also the firms that learn by doing, the rules they work under, and the history that keeps them on a track.

Fagerberg and colleagues, 2009, put it this way. They use a broad definition of the national innovation system, one that includes all learning and innovation across the economy. They trace the idea to Freeman, Lundvall and Nelson.

Why does the course begin here? Because the exam begins here. The first question of December 2024 asked what you understand by innovation system, sustainable transformation, agency and institutions. This theme gives you two of those four terms, and a Norwegian case that carries the rest.

There is a second voice in the theme. Fløysand and colleagues, 2013, describe innovation as a meeting of actors, objects and ideas. They call it the trinity of innovation. It is a useful reminder that a system is made of people, things and rules, not only of statistics.

Remember: a national system is the whole economy's way of learning, and Norway is the case the course uses.

## 2. The key ideas
<!-- readings: Fagerberg et al. 2009, pp. 431, 433, 439 -->

There are three ideas you need for the exam.

The first is that the system is broad. Learning happens in firms, in supplier networks and in public agencies, not only in research departments. That is why Norwegian firms can spend little on research and still innovate.

The second is institutions. Citing North, Fagerberg and colleagues, 2009, describe institutions as the rules of the game. They are costly to establish, but once they are in place they make interaction easier and they give scale advantages. That is also why they are hard to change.

The third is path dependency. Citing Arthur, the same authors explain that history favours some solutions and holds others back. A country that built ships and hydropower will find it easier to build offshore platforms than to build something entirely new.

Put the three together and you have the paper's argument. Norway grew fast with modest research spending because its system was broad, its institutions were stable and its history had prepared the ground. The authors are careful about the last point. Path dependency is not destiny. It shapes the options a country has, and policy can still push against it, though at a cost.

Remember: broad system, institutions as rules, and history that favours what already exists.

## 3. The model, step by step
<!-- readings: Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 439; Fløysand et al. 2013, PDF p. 10 -->

The paper offers a typology of three industrial development paths, and it is easy to remember.

First, the small scale decentralized path. Small firms invest little of their own money in innovation, and they rely on each other. This path dominated Norway until the early twentieth century.

Second, the large scale centralized path. Metals, electrochemistry and hydropower grew in big units, often financed from abroad.

Third, the knowledge intensive network based path. After the second world war, modernizers promoted research intensive firms in new sectors such as information technology.

The three paths coexist. That is the point. Norway did not replace one with another, and the mix is what makes the system diverse.

The second model comes from Fløysand and colleagues, 2013. They describe a practical triangle. A company works as the actor, its tools work as the objects, and its tasks work as the ideas. Technique links the company and the tools, production links the tools and the tasks, and rationale links the company and the tasks through profit.

Remember: three paths that coexist, and a triangle of company, tools and tasks.

## 4. Norway examples
<!-- readings: Fagerberg et al. 2009, pp. 434, 441 -->

Norway gives you two clean examples, and both are in the paper.

The first is petroleum. Citing the Dutch case, Fagerberg and colleagues, 2009, explain that a resource discovery can appreciate the currency and hurt manufacturing. They call that the Dutch disease. Norway avoided it. Public policy helped domestic suppliers in shipbuilding, engineering and services grow with the petroleum industry instead of being pushed out by it.

The second is the concession laws. Early rules on waterfalls became the model for national control of offshore oil and gas decades later. That is path dependency in its clearest form: an institution built for one industry was reused for another.

A third example is fish farming. Disease control was mastered without a large corporate laboratory, which shows how much learning happens outside the places we usually count.

Remember: petroleum suppliers instead of the Dutch disease, and waterfall rules reused for oil and gas.

## 5. How the exam asks about it
<!-- readings: Fagerberg et al. 2009, p. 431; Class 9, slide 38 -->

Two questions fit this theme.

The first is the concept question of December 2024. It asks what you understand by innovation system, sustainable transformation, agency and institutions. This theme gives you two of the four terms, and the other themes supply the rest. Start with the broad definition, then institutions as rules, then path dependency.

The second is the lecturer's own example question from Class 9. It asks how innovation systems promote or inhibit sustainable transformation. Here the answer turns on direction. A system that keeps building on what it already has will promote transformation only if the old path happens to be green.

A good move in both answers is to name the source. Say that Fagerberg and colleagues, 2009, use a broad definition, and say what that definition lets you see. Then show it in Norway.

There is also a short move that earns marks. Say what the system does not explain. A national system tells you why a country learns in its own way, but it says little about a single technology or a single region. That is why the course adds the other four systems, and why a comparison is often the strongest ending.

Remember: define broadly, name institutions and path dependency, and show the Norwegian case.
